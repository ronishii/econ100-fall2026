OUTPUTThe reading

GDP: the value of what the economy produces

Gross domestic product (GDP) is the market value of all final goods and services produced within a country in a year.

Each part of the definition decides what counts. GDP uses market value, so it adds things up at their prices. It counts final goods only, so nothing is counted twice. It counts what is produced this year, so a resale of an old good adds nothing.

What GDP counts

  • A new bicycle sold to a household this year
  • A haircut, a doctor’s visit, and other services
  • A new house built this year
  • Government purchases, such as a new school

What GDP leaves out

  • Flour that a bakery buys to bake bread. The bread is the final good, and its price already includes the flour.
  • A used car sold this year. It was counted in the year it was made.
  • A Social Security check. It is a transfer of income, not a payment for something produced.
  • Dinner cooked at home. No market sale takes place.
Checkpoint — commit before you reveal

A furniture maker buys $400 of lumber and sells the table it makes to a household for $1,000. How much does this add to GDP?

$1,000. The table is the final good. The $400 of lumber is an intermediate good, and its value is already part of the table’s $1,000 price. Counting both would count the lumber twice.

History: Before the 1930s, the U.S. government did not measure the output of the whole economy. During the Great Depression, Simon Kuznets produced the first official estimates of national output, for 1929–1932, and they were sent to the U.S. Senate in January 1934. In 2025, U.S. GDP was about $30.9 trillion.

GDP counts final goods and services, at market prices, in the year they are produced.
Instructor notes

Kuznets’s estimates were of national income, the forerunner of GNP and GDP (BEA, Survey of Current Business, August 2020). 2025 GDP: BEA via FRED series GDPA, $30,861 billion.

Nominal GDP and real GDP

GDP can rise for two reasons: the economy produces more, or prices rise. To separate the two reasons, economists measure GDP in two ways.

Nominal GDP

Nominal GDP values each year’s output at that year’s prices.

Real GDP

Real GDP values each year’s output at the prices of one base year. Because the prices are held fixed, real GDP changes only when the quantity of output changes.

A town that makes only pizzas: nominal GDP and real GDP, with Year 1 as the base year
YearPizzas madePriceNominal GDPReal GDP (Year 1 prices)
1100$10 a pizza100 pizzas × $10 = $1,000100 pizzas × $10 = $1,000
2100$12 a pizza100 pizzas × $12 = $1,200100 pizzas × $10 = $1,000

From Year 1 to Year 2, nominal GDP rose 20 percent: ($1,200 − $1,000) / $1,000 = 0.20. Real GDP did not change, because the town made the same 100 pizzas. The whole rise in nominal GDP came from the higher price.

Checkpoint — commit before you reveal

In Year 3, the town makes 110 pizzas, and the price stays at $12 a pizza. What are nominal GDP and real GDP in Year 3?

Nominal GDP is $1,320, and real GDP is $1,100. Nominal GDP is 110 pizzas × $12 = $1,320. Real GDP, at Year 1 prices, is 110 pizzas × $10 = $1,100. Real GDP is 10 percent above Year 1, because the town made 10 percent more pizzas.

To know whether the economy produced more, use real GDP.
RECESSIONSThe reading

The business cycle and recessions

The four phases

Real GDP rises over the long run, but not smoothly. In an expansion, real GDP rises until it reaches a peak. Then it falls during a recession until it reaches a trough, and a new expansion begins.

What counts as a recession

A recession is a significant fall in economic activity that spreads across the economy and lasts more than a few months. A common rule of thumb is two quarters in a row of falling real GDP. In the United States, the National Bureau of Economic Research dates recessions. The two most recent ran from December 2007 to June 2009 and from February to April 2020.

Checkpoint — commit before you reveal

During a recession, what usually happens to the unemployment rate, and why?

It rises. Firms sell less, so they produce less and need fewer workers. Some firms lay workers off, and fewer firms hire. That is why the unemployment rate jumped in the 2020 recession.

A recession is a fall in real GDP that spreads across the economy, and unemployment rises during it.
JOBSThe reading

Who counts as unemployed

Each month, the Bureau of Labor Statistics (BLS) surveys about 60,000 households. It sorts each adult into one of three groups.

Employed

Worked for pay during the survey week, or has a job and was away from it.

Unemployed

Has no job, is available to work, and looked for work in the past four weeks.

Not in the labor force

Has no job and is not looking: retirees, full-time students who are not looking, people caring for family, and discouraged workers, who want a job but stopped looking because they expect not to find one.

The labor force is the employed plus the unemployed. The unemployment rate is the unemployed divided by the labor force, times 100. The labor force participation rate is the labor force divided by all adults, times 100.

A town of 4,000 adults: the labor force, the unemployment rate, and the participation rate
MeasureCalculationResult
Labor force2,760 employed + 240 unemployed3,000 people
Unemployment rate240 people / 3,000 people = 0.0808.0 percent
Participation rate3,000 people / 4,000 people = 0.7575 percent

The other 1,000 adults are not in the labor force. In each rate, people are divided by people, so the units cancel and the result is a ratio.

Checkpoint — commit before you reveal

Suppose 30 of the town’s unemployed people give up looking for work. What is the new unemployment rate?

About 7.1 percent. The 30 people leave the labor force, so the unemployed fall to 210 and the labor force falls to 2,970: 210 / 2,970 = 0.071. The rate fell, but no one found a job. That is why economists watch the participation rate along with the unemployment rate.

Divide by the labor force, not by all adults. People who are not looking for work are not unemployed.
JOBSThe reading

Three types of unemployment

Frictional

Frictional unemployment is people between jobs while they search, such as a graduate looking for a first job or a nurse who moved to a new city. Some is normal, because finding a good match takes time.

Structural

Structural unemployment is a mismatch between workers’ skills or locations and the jobs available, such as a bank teller whose job was replaced by an app. It can last a long time, because workers need new skills or must move.

Cyclical

Cyclical unemployment is job loss because the whole economy is in a recession and firms produce less. It rises in recessions and falls in expansions.

The natural rate of unemployment is the unemployment rate when only frictional and structural unemployment remain. Economists estimate that the U.S. natural rate has been about 4 to 5 percent in recent decades. An unemployment rate of zero is not a realistic goal, because people are always changing jobs.

Checkpoint — commit before you reveal

A coal mine closes for good, and its miners have skills that no other employer in the region needs. Which type of unemployment is this?

Structural. The miners’ skills no longer match the jobs available in the region. Their unemployment would continue even after the economy recovers from a recession.

Frictional and structural unemployment are always present. Cyclical unemployment rises and falls with the business cycle.
PRICESThe reading

The Consumer Price Index: the cost of a fixed basket

Inflation is a rise in the general level of prices. To measure that level, the BLS prices a fixed basket of the goods and services that urban households buy, every month. The Consumer Price Index (CPI) compares the basket’s cost with its cost in a base year.

CPI = (cost of the basket this year / cost of the basket in the base year) × 100

A three-item basket for one household for one month, with Year 1 as the base year
ItemQuantityYear 1 priceYear 1 costYear 2 priceYear 2 cost
Gasoline20 gallons$4.00 a gallon$80.00$4.50 a gallon$90.00
Movie tickets4 tickets$15.00 a ticket$60.00$15.00 a ticket$60.00
Bread20 loaves$3.00 a loaf$60.00$3.30 a loaf$66.00
The basket$200.00$216.00

Year 1 is the base year, so its CPI is $200.00 / $200.00 × 100 = 100.0. In Year 2, the CPI is $216.00 / $200.00 × 100 = 108.0. The dollars cancel, so the CPI has no unit. It is an index number.

The quantities are the same in both years. Only the prices change, so the change in the basket’s cost measures the change in prices alone.

The CPI holds the basket fixed and measures what happens to its cost.
Instructor notes

The published CPI weights thousands of items by what households spend on them, using the BLS Consumer Expenditure Survey. The base period of the published CPI-U is 1982–84 = 100.

PRICESThe reading

From the CPI to the inflation rate

The inflation rate is the percent change in the CPI from one year to the next: (CPI this year − CPI last year) / CPI last year × 100.

From Year 1 to Year 2, the inflation rate was (108.0 − 100.0) / 100.0 = 0.080, or 8.0 percent. The index points cancel, so the result is a ratio, and multiplying by 100 gives a percent.

In Year 3, gasoline costs $4.60 a gallon, a movie ticket costs $16.00, and bread costs $3.54 a loaf. The same basket costs $92.00 + $64.00 + $70.80 = $226.80. The CPI is $226.80 / $200.00 × 100 = 113.4.

Checkpoint — commit before you reveal

What was the inflation rate from Year 2 to Year 3?

5.0 percent. (113.4 − 108.0) / 108.0 = 0.050. A CPI of 113.4 does not mean that prices rose 13.4 percent this year. It means that prices are 13.4 percent above the base year. The change from last year is the inflation rate.

The real numbers

Measured by the annual average CPI, the U.S. inflation rate was 1.2 percent in 2020 and 8.0 percent in 2022. The 2022 rate was the highest since 1981.

The inflation rate is the percent change in the CPI from last year, not the CPI’s distance from 100.
Instructor notes

Source for 2020 and 2022: BLS CPI-U, annual average percent change. 1981 was 10.3 percent, and 1982 was 6.2 percent.

STAGFLATIONThe reading

Stagflation: prices and unemployment rise together

In most recessions, unemployment rises and inflation slows, because households and firms spend less. The 1970s did not follow that pattern.

What happened

In October 1973, Arab oil-exporting countries stopped oil shipments to the United States, and the price of oil rose to about four times its earlier level. Inflation reached 11.0 percent in 1974. The economy also fell into a recession, and unemployment rose from 4.9 percent in 1973 to 8.5 percent in 1975. A second oil shock brought the same pattern in 1979–1980.

The term

Stagflation is high inflation together with rising unemployment and stagnant or falling output. The word joins “stagnation” and “inflation.”

Checkpoint — commit before you reveal

Read the chart. Name one year in which both the inflation rate and the unemployment rate were above 7 percent.

1975, 1980, or 1981. In 1975, inflation was 9.1 percent and unemployment was 8.5 percent. In 1980, they were 13.5 and 7.1 percent. In 1981, they were 10.3 and 7.6 percent.

Next week: Week 11 explains how one event, such as a rise in the price of oil, can raise prices and unemployment at the same time. The explanation uses aggregate supply.

Stagflation is high inflation at the same time as falling output and rising unemployment.

WATCH FORWrap-up

Six common errors this week

Common Module 10 errors and the correction for each
The errorWhy it is wrongWhat to do instead
Counting an intermediate good or a used good in GDP. An intermediate good is already part of the final good’s price. A used good was counted when it was made. Count only final goods and services produced this year.
Reading a rise in nominal GDP as a rise in output. Nominal GDP also rises when prices rise. Use real GDP, which holds prices at the base year.
Dividing the unemployed by all adults. The unemployment rate is a share of the labor force. Divide by the employed plus the unemployed.
Counting people who are not looking for work as unemployed. Retirees, students who are not looking, and discouraged workers are not in the labor force. Check whether the person looked for work in the past four weeks.
Reading a CPI of 113.4 as 13.4 percent inflation this year. 113.4 compares this year with the base year, not with last year. Calculate the percent change from last year’s CPI.
Calling a rise in one price inflation. Inflation is a rise in the general level of prices. Look at the CPI, which prices the whole basket.

Keep the base year straight. Real GDP and the CPI both compare with a base year. Real GDP values output at base-year prices. The CPI compares the basket’s cost with its cost in the base year.
