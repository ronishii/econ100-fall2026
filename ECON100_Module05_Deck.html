READ MEStart here

What this week covers

This week's main result surprises many people the first time they see it. A country that makes less of every good per hour can still gain from trade, and so can the country that makes more of every good per hour. Both countries gain, and the reason is opportunity cost. We introduced opportunity cost in Week 1, and we used it in Week 3 to build the supply curve.

Part 1: who should make what

We start with two countries that each make two goods. We calculate what each country gives up to make each good. Then we see total output rise when each country makes the good that costs it less. Output rises even though no one works more hours and no one learns a new skill.

Part 2: how the gain is divided, and who loses

We start by finding the range of prices at which both countries will accept a trade. Then we see how trade lets each country consume more than it could produce alone. Next, we look at who loses from trade inside a country. We finish by separating the claims that evidence can settle from the claims that rest on values.

Predict first

A surgeon is also the fastest typist in her hospital. She types faster than her receptionist does. Should she type her own letters? Write one sentence before you read on.

No. An hour that the surgeon spends typing is an hour that she does not spend operating, so the opportunity cost of her typing is an operation. The opportunity cost of the receptionist's typing is much smaller. So the person who gives up less should type the letters, and that person is the receptionist.

Many people accept this reasoning about a hospital but reject it about two countries. In Part 1, we show that the reasoning is the same.

OPPORTUNITY COSTDay 1

What each good costs each country

To see why total output rose, ask what each country gives up to make each good. What a country gives up is the good's opportunity cost.

The method has four steps

  1. Write what 1 worker-hour makes. The country makes an amount of one good or an amount of the other good.
  2. Name the good gained and the good given up. To find the opportunity cost of a good, suppose that the country makes more of that good. The country gains that good, and it gives up the other good.
  3. Divide the amount given up by the amount gained.
  4. Write the units. The units are the good given up, per 1 unit of the good gained.

Worked example 1: Southland's opportunity cost of 1 bracelet

  1. One Southland worker-hour makes 2 bracelets or 6 paper cranes.
  2. We want the cost of a bracelet, so Southland moves 1 worker-hour from paper cranes to bracelets. Southland gains 2 bracelets. Southland gives up 6 paper cranes.
  3. 6 paper cranes given up / 2 bracelets gained = 3.
  4. One bracelet costs Southland 3 paper cranes. With the units written, the opportunity cost is 3 paper cranes per bracelet.

Check the answer. Southland gained 2 bracelets, and each bracelet cost 3 paper cranes. 2 × 3 = 6 paper cranes, which is the amount that Southland gave up.

Worked example 2: Northland's opportunity cost of 1 bracelet

  1. One Northland worker-hour makes 4 bracelets or 4 paper cranes.
  2. Northland moves 1 worker-hour from paper cranes to bracelets. Northland gains 4 bracelets. Northland gives up 4 paper cranes.
  3. 4 paper cranes given up / 4 bracelets gained = 1.
  4. One bracelet costs Northland 1 paper crane. With the units written, the opportunity cost is 1 paper crane per bracelet.

In other words, every bracelet that Southland makes removes 3 paper cranes from the total. Every bracelet that Northland makes removes only 1 paper crane.

Checkpoint — your turn

Calculate the opportunity cost of 1 paper crane in Southland, and then in Northland. The two goods switch places. Each country now gains paper cranes and gives up bracelets. Follow the four steps on paper, and write the units.

Southland: 1/3 of a bracelet per paper crane. Northland: 1 bracelet per paper crane.

Southland. One worker-hour makes 2 bracelets or 6 paper cranes. Southland moves 1 worker-hour from bracelets to paper cranes. It gains 6 paper cranes and gives up 2 bracelets. 2 bracelets given up / 6 paper cranes gained = 1/3. One paper crane costs Southland 1/3 of a bracelet.

Northland. One worker-hour makes 4 bracelets or 4 paper cranes. Northland gains 4 paper cranes and gives up 4 bracelets. 4 bracelets given up / 4 paper cranes gained = 1. One paper crane costs Northland 1 bracelet.

The opportunity cost of one good is the reciprocal of the opportunity cost of the other good. If 1 bracelet costs 3 paper cranes, then 1 paper crane costs 1/3 of a bracelet.

The table below collects the four opportunity costs.

So, bracelets cost less in Northland, and paper cranes cost less in Southland. Total output is largest when each good is made in the country where it costs less.

To find an opportunity cost, divide what the country gives up by what it gains, and write the units.

THE MECHANISMDay 1

Why total output rises

What did not change

  • No one worked more hours.
  • No one learned a new skill.
  • No one bought new equipment.
  • No new materials arrived.

What changed

Each good moved to the country with the lower opportunity cost. Southland stopped making bracelets, which cost it 3 paper cranes each. Northland stopped making paper cranes, which cost it 1 bracelet each.

In Week 1, the pin factory raised output in a different way: each worker became faster at a narrower task. Here, no one became faster. The gain comes only from having each good made by the producer with the lower opportunity cost.

Specialization and trade can increase the total amount of goods. Trade does not only move a fixed amount of goods between owners.
Instructor notes

Ask the “what did not change” list as four questions. Four answers of “no” in a row set up the last card.

Watch for “specialization makes people more efficient.” That is Adam Smith's mechanism, and it is not what happened here. No production rate changed.

BUILDDay 1

Build #1: the two countries, drawn

Here are the same numbers, drawn as two production possibilities frontiers (PPFs). Each country can produce any combination on or below its own frontier. These frontiers are straight lines because each country's opportunity cost is the same at every point.

  1. Draw the axes and label the units first. Bracelets go on the horizontal axis. Paper cranes go on the vertical axis. Both are measured per 12 worker-hours.
  2. Northland's frontier. With all 12 worker-hours on bracelets, Northland makes 48 bracelets. With all 12 worker-hours on paper cranes, Northland makes 48 paper cranes. In autarky, Northland is at the middle of its frontier: 24 bracelets and 24 paper cranes.
  3. Southland's frontier. With all 12 worker-hours on bracelets, Southland makes only 24 bracelets. With all 12 worker-hours on paper cranes, Southland makes 72 paper cranes. In autarky, Southland makes 12 bracelets and 36 paper cranes.
  4. The endpoints show absolute advantage. Northland can make more bracelets than Southland can: 48 against 24. Southland can make more paper cranes: 72 against 48. A country that can make more of a good with the same resources has an absolute advantage in that good. But absolute advantage does not tell us who should make which good.
  5. The slopes show opportunity cost. Along Northland's frontier, each additional bracelet costs 1 paper crane. Along Southland's frontier, each additional bracelet costs 3 paper cranes. These are the two opportunity costs that we calculated earlier. Southland's frontier is steeper because bracelets cost more in Southland. The country with the lower opportunity cost of a good has a comparative advantage in that good.
Step 1 of 5
The endpoints of a frontier show how much a country can make. The slope of a frontier shows what each good costs that country.
Instructor notes

Draw-it-yourself first: students plot both frontiers from the two production rates before you draw anything. Look for unlabeled axes.

Steps 4 and 5 are the pair that teaches. Between them, ask: “We found which country can make more of each good. Does that tell us who should make what?” Let students answer yes. Then show step 5.

DEFINITIONSDay 1

Absolute advantage and comparative advantage

Absolute advantage

A producer has an absolute advantage in a good when it can make more of the good with the same resources. Absolute advantage answers the question: who can make more?

Northland has an absolute advantage in bracelets: 48 against 24.

Absolute advantage does not decide who should make the good.

Comparative advantage

A producer has a comparative advantage in a good when its opportunity cost of making the good is lower. Comparative advantage answers the question: who gives up less?

Northland has a comparative advantage in bracelets: 1 bracelet costs Northland 1 paper crane and costs Southland 3 paper cranes.

Comparative advantage decides who should make the good.

In other words, absolute advantage compares the endpoints of the two frontiers, and comparative advantage compares their slopes.

Return to the surgeon from the start of Part 1. She has an absolute advantage in both surgery and typing, but she has a comparative advantage only in surgery. The receptionist has a comparative advantage in typing.

A common error this week is to answer a comparative-advantage question with absolute-advantage evidence. Here is an example: “Southland should make paper cranes because Southland can make more paper cranes.” That reasoning sometimes reaches the right answer, but only by coincidence. Calculate both opportunity costs and compare them every time.

Instructor notes

THE HARD CASEDay 1

One country has an absolute advantage in both goods

So far, each country has had an absolute advantage in one good. In many arguments about trade, however, one country is more productive at most goods. On this slide, we work through that harder case.

Checkpoint — work it before you reveal it

Northland gets better tools, and its output per worker-hour doubles. Northland now makes 8 bracelets or 8 paper cranes per worker-hour. Southland still makes 2 bracelets or 6 paper cranes per worker-hour. With 12 worker-hours, Northland can now make 96 bracelets against Southland's 24, and 96 paper cranes against Southland's 72. So Northland has an absolute advantage in both goods.

Should Northland now make both goods itself and stop trading?

Recalculate the two opportunity costs. Then commit to an answer before you reveal it.

No. The comparative advantages have not changed. One Northland worker-hour makes 8 bracelets or 8 paper cranes, so 1 bracelet still costs Northland 1 paper crane. One bracelet still costs Southland 3 paper cranes. Northland still has a comparative advantage in bracelets, and Southland still has a comparative advantage in paper cranes.

Here are the totals. In autarky, Northland makes 48 bracelets and 48 paper cranes, and Southland makes 12 bracelets and 36 paper cranes. Together they make 60 bracelets and 84 paper cranes. With specialization, Southland puts all 12 worker-hours on paper cranes and makes 72. Northland puts 9 worker-hours on bracelets and 3 worker-hours on paper cranes, and makes 72 bracelets and 24 paper cranes. Together they make 72 bracelets and 96 paper cranes. That is 12 more of each good, which is the same gain as before.

Northland still makes some paper cranes in this case, because Southland's 72 paper cranes alone are fewer than the 84 paper cranes that the two countries had in autarky.

Doubling Northland's productivity made Northland richer, but it did not remove the gain from trading with Southland.

Why the opportunity cost did not change. Absolute advantage compares amounts, and amounts change when technology changes. Comparative advantage compares opportunity costs. An opportunity cost is a ratio: paper cranes given up per bracelet. When both of Northland's amounts double, the ratio stays the same: 8 / 8 = 4 / 4 = 1 paper crane per bracelet.

Instructor notes

Work this on the board rather than from the slide. Write the new rates, have students recompute the two opportunity costs, and wait for someone to say that the ratio is the same. The optional board sequence is workshop\Week05_Board_Better_at_Everything.html.

If the class is confident, go straight to Ricardo. His example is the same case with historical numbers.

HISTORYDay 1

History: David Ricardo, 1817

David Ricardo published this example in 1817. He chose the harder case on purpose. Portugal needs fewer men than England to make cloth, and fewer men to make wine. So Portugal has an absolute advantage in both goods, and it seems to follow that Portugal should make both goods.

The table shows why that conclusion is wrong. Making 1 unit of wine uses 80 Portuguese men, who could have made 80 / 90 = 0.89 units of cloth. Making 1 unit of wine uses 120 English men, who could have made 120 / 100 = 1.2 units of cloth. Wine costs Portugal less cloth than it costs England, so Portugal has a comparative advantage in wine.

Checkpoint — your turn

The table gives the opportunity cost of wine. Calculate the opportunity cost of 1 unit of cloth in each country, measured in wine. Do your two numbers agree with the table's conclusion that England should make the cloth?

Write both divisions before you reveal the answer.

Yes. One unit of cloth costs England 100 / 120 = 0.83 units of wine. One unit of cloth costs Portugal 90 / 80 = 1.13 units of wine. Cloth costs England less wine, so England has a comparative advantage in cloth.

Portugal needs fewer men than England to make cloth, but it still gains by buying its cloth from England. Both countries gain when Portugal makes the wine, England makes the cloth, and they trade.

Where this idea came from. Ricardo wrote during a political argument over the Corn Laws, which restricted grain imports into Britain. Ricardo opposed the Corn Laws, and his example supported the case for freer trade. The idea is more than 200 years old, and it is still often misunderstood. We return to the politics of trade in Part 2.

Instructor notes

Do the two divisions on the board, 80 / 90 and 120 / 100, with the units written out. Students trust arithmetic they watched more than arithmetic they read.

Predict first

The two countries are going to trade bracelets for paper cranes. What is the most that Southland would pay for 1 bracelet, in paper cranes? What is the least that Northland would accept?

Southland would pay less than 3 paper cranes, and Northland would accept more than 1 paper crane. Southland can make a bracelet itself at a cost of 3 paper cranes, so it will not pay 3 or more. DAY 2Day 2

Part 1 showed the gain. Who gets it?

The result from Part 1 is widely accepted among economists: when two countries have different opportunity costs, specialization and trade can raise total output. Yet trade is also one of the most disputed subjects in politics. In Part 2, we explain how both statements can be true.

First: the terms of trade

We find which prices both countries accept, and which prices one country refuses.

Then: consumption beyond the frontier

We compare what each country consumes after trade with what it consumed in autarky.

Finally: winners, losers, and the argument

We ask who gains a little, who loses a lot, and which parts of the disagreement evidence can settle.

A caution before we start. It would be easy to conclude that everyone who objects to trade has misunderstood Ricardo. That conclusion is wrong. The gains from trade are real, and so are the losses, but they fall on different people. No model settles what should be done for the people who lose. In Part 2, you will learn to state accurately who gains, who loses, and by about how much. You will need those facts for any position that you argue.

TERMS OF TRADEDay 2

The terms of trade: which prices both countries accept

Why the price must be above 1

One bracelet costs Northland 1 paper crane to make. If Southland offers less than 1 paper crane for a bracelet, Northland does better by making its own paper cranes, so it refuses.

Why the price must be below 3

Southland can make a bracelet itself by giving up 3 paper cranes. If Northland asks for more than 3 paper cranes per bracelet, Southland does better by making its own bracelets, so it refuses.

Checkpoint — would they accept?

Northland offers 1 bracelet in exchange for 4 paper cranes. Does Southland accept?

Commit to an answer before you reveal it.

No. Southland can make a bracelet itself by giving up 3 paper cranes. Paying 4 paper cranes for a bracelet leaves Southland worse off than autarky, so Southland refuses and makes its own bracelets.

Now look at the other end of the range. Would Northland accept 1/2 of a paper crane for 1 bracelet? No. One bracelet costs Northland 1 paper crane to make, and 1/2 of a paper crane is less than Northland's opportunity cost of 1 bracelet. Above 3 paper cranes per bracelet, Southland refuses. Below 1 paper crane per bracelet, Northland refuses.

The terms of trade are the rate at which one good trades for the other. In this example, the terms of trade are the price of 1 bracelet, measured in paper cranes.

Both countries gain at any terms of trade between their two opportunity costs. The model gives a range, not one number. Where the price falls inside the range depends on bargaining: which country needs the trade more, how many other trading partners each country has, and how well each side negotiates. In HW5b, you will negotiate a price inside a range like this one.

Instructor notes

Ask the 4-paper-crane question and take a vote before revealing. If you ran the experiment, put the rates the pairs agreed on the number line. If a pair agreed on a rate outside the range, ask which country should have refused.

BUILDDay 2

Build #2: the two frontiers, with a trade

These are the same axes and the same two frontiers as in Build #1. This time, we add a trade, and we show what each country consumes after the trade.

  1. Autarky. Northland makes and consumes 24 bracelets and 24 paper cranes. Southland makes and consumes 12 bracelets and 36 paper cranes. Each point is on the country's own frontier, because a country in autarky can consume only what it produces.
  2. Specialization. Northland makes 48 bracelets and no paper cranes. Southland makes 72 paper cranes and no bracelets. Neither country wants to consume only one good, so they trade.
  3. The trade. The terms of trade are 2 paper cranes per bracelet. Northland sends 15 bracelets to Southland and receives 30 paper cranes.
  4. Northland's consumption. Northland now has 33 bracelets and 30 paper cranes. That point is beyond Northland's frontier, which means that Northland could not have produced this combination with its own 12 worker-hours.
  5. Southland's consumption. Southland now has 15 bracelets and 42 paper cranes, which is also beyond its frontier. Compared with autarky, Northland gained 9 bracelets and 6 paper cranes, and Southland gained 3 bracelets and 6 paper cranes. Both countries gained in both goods.
Step 1 of 5
A country's frontier limits what it can produce. With trade, the frontier does not limit what the country can consume.
Instructor notes

THE RESULTDay 2

Consumption beyond the frontier

Checkpoint

With trade, Northland consumes 33 bracelets and 30 paper cranes. Show with numbers that Northland could not have produced that combination with its own 12 worker-hours.

33 + 30 = 63, and Northland can make at most 48. Each bracelet costs Northland 1 paper crane, so the most Northland can make is 48 goods in total, in any combination. 33 + 30 = 63, which is more than 48. The combination is beyond Northland's frontier.

The same check works for Southland. Making 15 bracelets would cost Southland 45 paper cranes and leave it with 72 − 45 = 27 paper cranes. With trade, Southland has 42 paper cranes.

In Week 1, we said that the frontier is the limit of what a country can produce. That is still true. This week, we add that the frontier is not the limit of what a country can consume. In autarky, consumption equals production. With trade, consumption can be beyond the frontier.

With trade, a country produces on its frontier and consumes beyond it.
Instructor notes

Point back to the Week 1 frontier: “We said this line was the limit. It is the limit of production. It was never the limit of consumption.”

DEFINITIONSDay 2

Autarky, free trade, and trade barriers

Our two-country example compared two extremes: autarky and free trade. Actual trade policy is usually somewhere between them.

Autarky

The countries do not trade. Each country consumes only what it produces. In the example, the two countries together make 36 bracelets and 60 paper cranes.

Free trade

The countries trade with no legal barriers. Each country specializes according to its comparative advantage. In the example, the two countries together make 48 bracelets and 72 paper cranes.

Trade barriers

A tariff is a tax on an imported good. A quota is a legal limit on the quantity of a good that can be imported. Both move a country part of the way from free trade back toward autarky.

A tariff raises the price of the imported good inside the country that imposes it. Domestic producers of the same good can then charge more, so they gain. Domestic buyers pay more, so they lose. The government collects the tariff revenue.

In HW5c, you will work through an actual tariff: the tariff that the United States placed on imported washing machines in 2018. Economists measured what the tariff did to prices, to jobs, and to what buyers paid.

Instructor notes

Keep this short. The three definitions are what students need for the quiz and for HW5c. The supply-and-demand analysis of a tariff is not part of this module.

BOTH SIDESDay 2

Winners, losers, and why trade is disputed

“Both countries gain” is a statement about each country as a whole. Inside each country, however, trade creates winners and losers. The two groups differ in size and in how much each person gains or loses.

The gains are spread across many people

Imports lower prices for millions of buyers. A household might save a few dollars a month on clothes and appliances and not notice. The total gain is large, but each person's share is small.

People rarely organize politically over a small saving.

The losses are concentrated on a few people

A factory that competes with imports closes in one town. Its workers lose their wages, and the factory may be the town's largest employer. In the model, the total loss is smaller than the total gain, but each person's loss is large.

Those workers have a strong reason to organize and to contact their representatives.

Compensation. When the total gains are larger than the total losses, the winners could pay the losers enough to cover the losses and still come out ahead. Economists often make this argument. Whether the compensation is actually paid is a separate question. Historically, it has often not been paid, or it has covered only part of the loss. That difference between the model and actual policy belongs in any argument about trade.

What this slide says and does not say. It does not say that trade is bad. It does not say that objections to trade are mistaken. It says that the model answers one question: does total output rise? The model leaves a second question open: what is owed to the people who lose? Keep the two questions separate.

Instructor notes

Present both columns evenly and do not give your own view. If asked directly, say that the total gains are well established and that what to do about the losses is a values question.

SORTDay 2

Positive and normative claims about trade

In Week 4, we separated positive questions from normative questions about price controls. Arguments about trade mix the two kinds of claim, often in the same sentence.

A positive claim says what is, or what will happen. Evidence can settle it, even when the evidence is hard to collect.

A normative claim says what ought to be. It rests on a judgment about what matters, and evidence alone cannot settle it.

Sort these six claims about a tariff on imported steel. Write “positive” or “normative” for each claim before you reveal the answer.

  1. “Removing this tariff would lower the price of steel in this country.”
  2. “Removing this tariff would cost about 3,000 jobs in the steel industry.”
  3. “Those 3,000 jobs matter more than the savings to steel buyers.”
  4. “Total output in both countries would rise.”
  5. “A country should be able to make its own steel, whatever it costs.”
  6. “The workers who lose their jobs should be compensated from the gains.”
Check your sort

Ask one question about each claim: could evidence settle it?

Claims 1, 2, and 4 are positive. Each one is a prediction about the world. The numbers may be hard to measure, and economists may disagree about their size, but evidence can still settle these claims.

Claims 3, 5, and 6 are normative. Each one rests on a judgment: whose interests count for more, whether self-sufficiency is worth its cost, what is owed to the people a policy harms. Data cannot settle these claims, and disagreeing about them is not a factual error.

Claim 2 is often labeled wrong. A job-loss number sounds like an argument against removing the tariff, so people label the claim normative. But it is a factual prediction, and it can be checked. What should be done about the job losses is the normative question.

In many arguments about trade, two people disagree about values while both say that they disagree about facts.

Spend the time on claims 2 and 4. Ask: “Could you check this? How?” HW5c uses a different case and different claims, so the class key does not answer it.

DEPTHDay 2

Four assumptions in the two-country model

1 · Workers can move between jobs at no cost

The model moves worker-hours from paper cranes to bracelets at no cost. In reality, retraining takes time and money, some workers never retrain, and the new jobs are often in a different town.

2 · Opportunity costs are constant

The straight frontiers mean that the 100th bracelet costs the same as the 1st. In Week 3, we drew a bowed frontier that showed increasing opportunity cost. With increasing opportunity cost, countries specialize partly and not completely, which is what real countries do.

3 · There are only two countries and two goods

Adding more countries and more goods does not change the reasoning. It does mean that a country's comparative advantage can change as other countries develop, so trade patterns change over time.

4 · Only output matters

National security, environmental standards, working conditions, and dependence on a single supplier are real concerns. The model says nothing about these concerns, but its silence does not mean that they are unimportant.

Why we still use the simple model. The model's main result generally holds when the assumptions are relaxed: differences in opportunity cost create potential gains from trade. Relaxing the assumptions changes the size of the gains, how quickly they arrive, and who bears the costs. Knowing which results depend on which assumptions is an important part of using any economic model.

Instructor notes

Assumption 2 deserves the most time. It connects to Week 3's bowed frontier, and it explains something students notice: no country makes only one good.

If time is short, this slide can be left to the reading. Keep the positive-and-normative sort.

WATCH FORDay 2

Six common errors this week

Common Module 5 errors and the correction for each
The errorWhy it is wrongWhat to do instead
“They should make it because they can make more of it.” That is absolute advantage. It answers a different question, and it does not decide who should make the good. Calculate both countries' opportunity cost of the good and compare the two numbers.
“If one country has an absolute advantage in both goods, trade cannot help.” This is the case Ricardo wrote about, and both countries still gain. Check whether the two opportunity costs differ. If they differ, there are gains from trade.
Inverting an opportunity cost without noticing. If 1 bracelet costs 3 paper cranes, then 1 paper crane costs 1/3 of a bracelet, not 3 bracelets. Write out both opportunity costs, with units, before you answer.
Treating the terms of trade as one correct number. Both countries gain at any price between the two opportunity costs. The model gives a range, not a single price. Find both opportunity costs first. They are the two ends of the range.
“Both countries gain, so no one loses.” Each country gains in total. Particular industries and particular workers inside a country can lose a great deal. Say whether you are talking about the country as a whole or about people inside it.
Calling a job-loss forecast an opinion. A prediction about how many jobs will be lost is a positive claim, because it can be checked. Ask “could evidence settle this?” If yes, the claim is positive, whatever it implies.

Answer with units. “2 paper cranes per bracelet” is an answer. “2” is not an answer, because a question with two goods has two possible units.
