👋 Introduction

By Sunday you should be able to take a decision — yours, a company's, a government's — and name what it actually cost. Not the price tag. The thing that was given up to get it.

That is the week. Economics is the study of choice under scarcity: there is not enough of anything to go around, so every choice closes a door. From that one fact the course builds four ideas — scarcity, rationality, self-interest, and the economic problem of what gets produced, how, and for whom.

Adam Smith's pin factory — each worker a different station, each station a different task. From Diderot & d'Alembert's Encyclopédie (1762), public domain, via Wikimedia Commons. Thursday we rebuild it with paper and four volunteers.

You also meet the three pictures economists reach for first: the circular flow, the budget constraint, and the production possibilities frontier. This week you are not asked to operate any of them. You are asked to read one — say what it shows, name what it assumes, and say what it leaves out. That is the harder skill, and it is the one the course actually teaches.

Tuesday's math primer covers every bit of math this course will ever use. There is no math prerequisite. If you can work out a restaurant tip, you already have what you need.

💡 Big idea: Every choice has a cost, and the cost is rarely the number on the receipt.

🎯 What to Pay Attention To

🧠 The math you need, all of it (Tuesday's primer)

  • Reading a chart, in four questions: what is on each axis, what are the units and scale (does the Y axis start at zero?), what does one point say, and only then — what is the pattern?
  • Percentage change: (new − old) / old × 100. Always divide by the old number.
  • “Per” means divide, and units matter: 240 miles / 8 gallons = 30 miles/gallon. Almost every number carries a unit — know what it is before you use it.

Part 1 (Tuesday, Aug 25): First Principles — and All the Math

  • What economics is, and scarcity: the study of choice under scarcity, not the study of money. Time is the scarce resource people forget to count.
  • Opportunity cost, felt before it is defined: the workshop puts a number on what it costs you to sit in this room for 85 minutes.
  • Specialization and the division of labor: why Smith's pin factory out-produces lone generalists, and why specialists have to trade.
  • Microeconomics vs. macroeconomics: tell whether a question is about one market or the whole economy.
  • How economists use models: a model simplifies on purpose, the way a subway map does. The first one, the circular flow, is built loop by loop.
  • Economic systems: traditional, command, and market answer the same three questions differently, and every real economy is a mix.

Part 2 (Thursday, Aug 27): The Assumptions Underneath

  • Specialization, tested live: class opens with a four-student assembly line, and we find out whether specializing really wins.
  • Rationality: a working assumption economists make about how people choose. Not a compliment, and not a claim that anyone is always right.
  • Self-interest: also an assumption, and not the same thing as selfishness — people's goals routinely include family, community, and causes.
  • The economic problem: what gets produced, how, and for whom. Every economy in history has answered these three, and the systems above are three different machines for answering them.
  • Thinking at the margin, and sunk costs: decide one-more-or-one-less, and leave money already spent out of it.
  • The two big pictures: the budget constraint and the production possibilities frontier, each read the same way — what it shows, what it assumes, what it leaves out.
  • Positive vs. normative: tell a testable description apart from a value judgment. This is the habit we apply to the news all semester.
