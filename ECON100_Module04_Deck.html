No one is in charge of the price of coffee. No committee meets and no law sets it. Yet walk into any two shops on the same street and the prices are within a few cents of each other. How does that happen? Write one sentence before you read on.

Every buyer and every seller acts on their own private number, and only one price is consistent with all of those numbers at once. Set the price too high and shops are left holding coffee nobody bought. Set it too low and there are lines and empty shelves. Either way, somebody has a reason to change their price, and they do.

THE MARKETDay 1

The apple market — how it works

Thirty-six people. Eighteen hold a BUYER card and eighteen hold a SELLER card, each with one private number on it. Nobody is told a price.

🍎 If your card says BUYER

Your card shows a number: what one apple is worth to you, in dollars. If you buy an apple for less than that number, you keep the difference. Buy for more and you have lost money. Buy nothing and you end with zero, which is better than a loss.

The number is private. Nobody else sees the card.

🌱 If your card says SELLER

Your card shows a number: what one apple costs you to supply. Sell above that number and you keep the difference. Sell below it and you have lost money. Sell nothing and you end with zero.

That number is private too.

  1. Buyers and sellers find each other and haggle out loud.
  2. A pair agrees on a price, or walks away. Nobody is required to trade. One apple per person, per round.
  3. Every agreed price goes on a board the moment the pair shakes on it — the price only, never a card number.
  4. Three rounds, same cards each round.

Before the next slide: no one in this market can see more than one card, and no one is in charge. Will the round-3 prices be scattered from $1 to $9, or bunched together? Decide before you go on.

§3.1Day 1

Build #1: the apple market, drawn

Same numbers as the table, now on a graph: the demand curve from Week 2 and the supply curve from Week 3 on one set of axes.

  1. Axes and units first. Price in dollars per apple runs up the side, 0 to 9. Number of apples runs along the bottom, 0 to 18. Every point below comes from the cards.
  2. The buyers. At $8 only 6 people want an apple; at $2, all 18 do. Connect them and you have the market's demand curve — downward-sloping, as in Week 2.
  3. The sellers. At $2 only 6 people will part with an apple; at $8, all 18 will. That is the market's supply curve — upward-sloping, as in Week 3.
  4. Try a price that is too high. At $7, sellers offer 16 apples and buyers want only 9. Seven apples sit unsold. A seller holding one lowers their asking price, and the price falls.
  5. Try a price that is too low, then stop where they cross. At $3, buyers want 16 and sellers offer only 9 — seven buyers go home without one, and some of them offer more. The only price with no leftover apples and no leftover buyers is $5, at 12 apples. That is the equilibrium.
Step 1 of 5
Equilibrium is not a fair price or a good price. It is the only price with nothing left over on either side.

§3.1Day 1

Three terms, defined

Equilibrium price

The one price at which the quantity buyers want to buy equals the quantity sellers want to sell. On a graph, the height of the crossing point.

In the apple market: $5 per apple.

Equilibrium quantity

The amount actually bought and sold at that price. On a graph, the horizontal position of the crossing point.

In the apple market: 12 apples.

Equilibrium

The state in which the price stops moving: not because everyone is satisfied, but because no buyer and no seller has a reason to change what they are doing.

Latin: equal weight.

Careful with the word. “Equilibrium” is a description, not a judgment. A market can be in equilibrium and still leave people hungry, houses empty, or workers unemployed. The word claims only that the price has stopped moving. Whether we like where it stopped is a different question, a normative one, and Part 2 takes it up.

Carry the unit. “Equilibrium quantity” is a quantity of goods — 12 apples, 600 million gallons — and “equilibrium price” is a price. “12” is not an answer to a question that asked for a price. Write the unit every time.

Equilibrium is the price no buyer or seller has a reason to move away from.
Instructor notes

The normative caveat is what stops students hearing the whole course as an endorsement. Say it once, clearly, and point forward to Part 2.

§3.1Day 1

What happens at any other price

🔻 Above equilibrium: a SURPLUS

Quantity supplied exceeds quantity demanded. Also called excess supply. Sellers are left holding stock, so some of them cut their price, and the price falls toward equilibrium.

At $1.80: 680 offered, 500 wanted, surplus of 180 million gallons.

🔼 Below equilibrium: a SHORTAGE

Quantity demanded exceeds quantity supplied. Also called excess demand. Buyers compete for the smaller quantity, so some of them offer more, and the price rises toward equilibrium.

At $1.20: 700 wanted, 550 offered, shortage of 150 million gallons.

Two traps. First: a shortage is not the same as scarcity. Everything is scarce; a shortage exists only at a particular price that is below equilibrium. Second: “surplus” and “shortage” each describe a gap in quantity at a stated price, so you measure one horizontally, along the price line, between the two curves. A vertical distance on this graph is a difference in price, not a quantity.

§3.3Day 1

What happens when something changes: four steps, every time

Most questions about a market have the same form: something happens; what happens to price and quantity? There is a procedure. Use it every time, including when you think you can see the answer.

  1. Draw the market before anything happens. Both curves, labeled, equilibrium marked.
  2. Decide which curve moves. Did the news change something about buyers or about sellers? This is the same sorting question as in Week 3, and it is still the hard step.
  3. Decide which way it moves. More at every price, or less at every price? Say it as a sentence about quantity at every price; do not think “up” or “down.”
  4. Read the new crossing. Compare the new price and quantity with the old ones. Answer in both, with units.
Work it now — the whole four steps

A long heatwave ruins a large share of this year's apple harvest. What happens to the price of apples, and to the quantity traded? Do all four steps before revealing — especially step 2.

Step 1: the apple market, demand and supply crossing at $5 and 12 apples.

Step 2: the heatwave changed growing conditions, which is something about sellers. Supply moves. Demand does not, because nothing about buyers changed.

Step 3: supply shifts LEFT — fewer apples offered at every price.

Step 4: the new crossing sits higher up and further left: price rises, quantity falls.

You did not need to know how large the harvest loss was, or what the new price is in dollars. The four steps give a direction, and a direction is usually what the question asks for.

The common error: “apples got expensive, so demand fell.” The price rose because supply fell; quantity demanded then fell as a movement along a demand curve that did not move. One curve shifted, the other did not. If you find yourself shifting both curves for a single event, check for this mistake.

HISTORYDay 1

Alfred Marshall and the two blades of the scissors

For most of the nineteenth century economists argued about what determines the value of a thing. One camp said cost of production — a coat is worth what it took to make. The other said usefulness to the buyer — a coat is worth what someone will give for it. Each side had a point, and the dispute went on for decades.

“We might as reasonably dispute whether it is the upper or the under blade of a pair of scissors that cuts a piece of paper, as whether value is governed by utility or cost of production.”

Alfred Marshall, Principles of Economics, Book V, Chapter III (1890; quoted from the public-domain eighth edition, 1920)

Marshall's answer was the diagram you have just built. Demand is one blade and supply is the other; asking which one “really” sets the price is asking the wrong question. The price is where they meet, and you need both.

Why it mattered

It settled the dispute by showing that each side had been describing one blade. It is also the origin of the supply-and-demand diagram in the form you now use. Marshall put price on the vertical axis, and economists have kept that convention for well over a century.

Where you have already met this

Week 3 closed on the value-versus-cost dispute. This is its resolution. It also explains why diamonds cost more than water: water as a whole is far more useful, but one more glass of it is worth very little, and price is set by the last unit traded, the one at the margin.

Cost shapes the supply curve, usefulness shapes the demand curve, and the price is where they meet.
Instructor notes

Read the quotation out loud; it is short and it works spoken.

The diamond–water line is worth thirty seconds because it previews marginal thinking, which comes back in Week 6.

§3.4Day 2

Price ceilings: a legal maximum

What the law says

“Nobody may charge more than $500.” A price ceiling is a legal maximum price.

Without it, rent would have risen to $600 and 17,000 apartments would have been rented.

What happens

At $500 owners are willing to supply 15,000 units. Renters want 19,000. That is a shortage of 4,000 apartments, and it does not go away, because the price rise that would normally clear it is illegal.

Notice what did not happen. The law capped the price. It did not, and could not, cap demand. Nineteen thousand people still want an apartment. What changes is how they compete for the fifteen thousand that exist: waiting lists, side payments, personal connections, and units whose condition is allowed to slide. Outlawing the price does not end the competition; it changes the form of the competition to one that is harder to see and usually less fair.

Instructor notes

Commit before the graph: “the city caps rent at the old price. Do more or fewer apartments get rented than before the cap?” Expect a large “more” vote; resolve it with the figure.

The “competition changes form” paragraph is the one they remember. Give it its own beat and ask for examples from their own experience of looking for housing in San Diego County.

§3.4Day 2

Price floors: a legal minimum

What the law says

“Nobody may pay less than $5 per pound.” A price floor is a legal minimum price, usually meant to protect the seller's income.

The most familiar price floor in your life is a minimum wage — a legal minimum price for an hour of labor. The diagram is the same one; the market is people's time.

What happens

At $5 growers offer 90 million lb. Buyers only want 55 million. A surplus of 35 million lb piles up, and somebody has to store it, dump it, export it, or be paid by the government to take it.

The two cases mirror each other. A ceiling holds price down, so quantity demanded is large, quantity supplied is small, and the result is a shortage. A floor holds price up, so quantity supplied is large, quantity demanded is small, and the result is a surplus. In both cases the short side of the market sets the quantity: the amount traded is whichever quantity is smaller. That rule answers many quiz and exam questions on its own.

Instructor notes

Do not turn the minimum-wage sentence into a debate; name it as the familiar example, note that the labor market has features this simple diagram leaves out, and move on. It comes back in Week 9.

The short-side rule goes on the board and stays there.

§3.4Day 2

The question to ask first: does it bind?

A price control on the wrong side of equilibrium does nothing. The market carries on as it would have, and the law has no effect. Before you analyze any control, ask whether it binds.

🔻 A CEILING binds from below

A legal maximum only matters if it is below equilibrium. Set a maximum price for coffee at $50 a cup and nothing happens, because nobody was going to charge that anyway.

Binding ceiling → shortage.

🔼 A FLOOR binds from above

A legal minimum only matters if it is above equilibrium. Set a minimum price for coffee at 10¢ a cup and nothing happens, because nobody was going to charge that little.

Binding floor → surplus.

Checkpoint — this is the trap

A market is in equilibrium at $5. Two towns pass laws. Town A sets a legal maximum of $3. Town B sets a legal minimum of $3. Which town's market changes?

A only, B only, both, or neither — commit before you click.

Town A only. A maximum of $3 sits below the $5 equilibrium, so it binds: price is forced down, sellers offer less, and a shortage appears.

Town B's minimum of $3 also sits below equilibrium, but a minimum below the going price has no effect. The market was already at $5, above the $3 minimum, and it stays there.

Same number, same market, different result, because one is a ceiling and one is a floor. Read the word before you read the number.

A ceiling below equilibrium binds. A floor above equilibrium binds. Any other control has no effect.
Instructor notes

This is the key slide of Part 2. Have everyone commit to A, B, both or neither before any discussion. Expect a large “both” vote on the first pass; that is the misconception and it is worth the time.

Board: two axes side by side, same equilibrium, the same horizontal line at $3 on each, labeled MAXIMUM on one and MINIMUM on the other. The identical picture with opposite consequences is what makes it stick.

§3.5Day 2

Build #2: the apple market with a ceiling on it

Same axes as Build #1, same thirty-six cards. Count, in whole dollars and one apple at a time, what a price ceiling does.

  1. The market, left alone. Twelve apples trade at $5. Add up what every buyer gained (their card value minus the $5 they paid) and you get $29. Do the same for the sellers and you get $29. Together, $58 of gains from twelve voluntary trades.
  2. Now a ceiling at $3. Nobody may charge more. Draw the line.
  3. How many apples get made? Only the sellers whose card is $3 or less will part with one — that is 9 sellers. Sixteen buyers want one. Nine is what there is, and nine is what trades. (The short side of the market sets the quantity, as before.)
  4. The transfer. Those 9 buyers each pay $2 less than they would have — +$18 to buyers. Those 9 sellers each receive $2 less — −$18 to sellers. That gain is real, and it is why the law has supporters. But it is a transfer: nothing was created or destroyed; $18 moved from one side to the other.
  5. The part that is destroyed. Apples 10, 11 and 12 never get made. Apple 10 was worth $6 to its buyer and cost its seller $4 — $2 of gain that no longer happens. Same for apple 11. Apple 12 was worth $5 to both, so losing it costs nothing. Total: $4 of gains that nobody gets. Economists call it deadweight loss.
Step 1 of 5

A caveat, and it is on the quiz. The $4 figure assumes the 9 apples go to the 9 buyers who value them most. Under a ceiling there is no price to sort buyers, so the apples go to whoever waits in line, knows the seller, or gets lucky, and the loss is larger than $4. Every number on this slide is the best case for the policy.

Instructor notes

Live option. The counting workshop (workshop\Week04_Workshop_Counting_the_Loss.html) has them add up the buyers' and sellers' gains at $5 in pairs from the card list, then cap the price at $3 and count again. Do not do the arithmetic for them; the counting is the lesson.

Step 4 is the political heart of the slide. Say clearly: the transfer is real, it is large, and it is why people vote for this. Then step 5: and separately, $4 evaporates.

The rationing caveat must be said out loud, not left on the slide. The commonest wrong answer for the loss is $18 — the transfer counted as a loss. Ask who is holding the eighteen dollars.

§3.5Day 2

The same count, apple by apple

Consumer surplus

What a buyer would have paid, minus what they did pay — the rectangles above the price line. At $5, they add to $29.

Producer surplus

What a seller received, minus the least they would have accepted — the rectangles below the price line. At $5, $29.

Social surplus

The two added together: $58. It is largest at the equilibrium quantity, and any policy that changes the quantity traded makes it smaller.

The same three numbers, under the ceiling. Panel (b) has buyers gaining $45 and sellers $9, for $54 together. Buyers are better off than before, $45 against $29, because $18 moved to them. Sellers are worse off by more than $18, because they also lose the gains they had on the three apples that no longer trade. The difference between the two changes is the $4 that went to nobody.

Why count instead of measuring an area. Textbooks draw these as triangles and compute areas. That works, but it hides what is being added up. Every dollar of surplus belongs to a specific person on a specific trade, and you can count them one at a time, which is what Build #2 did. The triangle is a shortcut for the counting.

Surplus is a sum of individual gains, one per trade. Drawn on a graph, the sum looks like an area.
BOTH SIDESDay 2

So why does anyone pass these laws?

If price controls reduce total surplus, why are they so common? Because the analysis above is not the whole argument.

The case for

  • The transfer is real and it is large. In the apple market, $18 moved to buyers and only $4 disappeared. If you care more about who has the money than about the size of the total, that trade can be worth making.
  • Equilibrium says nothing about need. A market clears at the equilibrium price whether the people priced out are choosing to go without or cannot afford to buy.
  • Some markets are not textbook markets. Where one side has far more power than the other, the simple diagram understates what is going on.
  • Short-run emergencies. Anti-gouging laws after a disaster are price ceilings, and people support them for reasons that go beyond a misunderstanding of the diagram.

The case against

  • The shortage does not go away. It reappears as lines, waiting lists, worse quality, or personal connections, and those costs usually fall hardest on the people with the least time, money and connections.
  • The deadweight loss is invisible. Nobody protests about an apartment that was never built, so the cost never enters the political argument.
  • The $4 is the best case. Real rationing is worse than the assumption behind it.
  • There is usually a cheaper way to help the same people: a direct subsidy or voucher makes the same transfer without preventing the trades.

What economics contributes here is the cost, not the verdict. Whether the $18 transfer is worth the $4 loss is a value judgment, and your answer may reasonably differ from mine. That the $4 exists is not a matter of opinion. A claim that a price control is free is wrong; a claim that it therefore cannot be worth doing skips the value judgment.

Instructor notes

Do not editorialize. Present both columns evenly and let the disagreement live. If asked directly what you think, it is fine to say that the diagram does not settle it and that reasonable people weight the transfer differently.

The voucher-versus-control point is the one economists agree on, and it is worth naming as such.

§5.1Day 2

Elasticity: how much, not just which way

Every week so far you have answered which direction. Elasticity answers how much. It is one question: when the price changes by some percentage, what percentage does the quantity change by?

Elastic

Quantity moves more, in percentage terms, than price did. Buyers cut back sharply.

Price +25%, quantity −40% → elastic.

Inelastic

Quantity moves less, in percentage terms, than price did. Buyers mostly keep buying.

Price +25%, quantity −8% → inelastic.

Unit elastic

The two percentages are equal. The boundary between the other two.

Price +25%, quantity −25%.

Elasticity is not slope. It is tempting to say “flatter means more elastic,” and on one fixed pair of axes that is roughly true. But change the units on either axis and the same curve gets flatter or steeper without a single buyer changing their mind. Percentages have no units, which is why economists use them. If you are judging elasticity by eye from a graph, stop and find the two percentages instead.

Instructor notes

Lead with the parking permit, because they live it: “if the permit went from $60 to $75 next term, how many of you would stop driving?” Few hands. Then the taco truck: “if one truck raised tacos from $4 to $5 and the other five did not?” Nearly all. The idea is theirs; you only have to name it.

Do not introduce the midpoint formula. Percentages compared as two numbers is the whole requirement in this course.

§5.3Day 2

The revenue test

Total revenue is price × quantity. Raise the price and you earn more on each sale but make fewer sales. Which effect is larger? Elasticity decides.

Predict first

You own the taco truck. You are considering raising the price from $4 to $5. There are five other taco trucks on the same block. Does your revenue go up or down? Commit before you look.

Down, from $2,000 to $1,500. With five substitutes on the same block, demand for your tacos is elastic. You gained $1 on each of the 300 sales you kept and lost 200 sales at $4 each.

Now change one thing: you sell campus parking permits, and you are the only seller. The same 25% price rise sends revenue up, from $120,000 to $138,000. The arithmetic is the same; what changed is how easily buyers can walk away.

§5.1Day 2

Four things decide how much buyers cut back

1 · Substitutes — by far the biggest

How easily can a buyer get the same satisfaction somewhere else? Demand for one brand of bottled water on a shelf of ten is highly elastic. Demand for water itself is not.

This is why a brand is almost always more elastic than the product.

2 · Necessity or luxury

You can skip a concert. You cannot skip insulin. Necessities are inelastic; things you could do without are elastic.

OpenStax reports estimates of about 2.27 for restaurant meals and about 0.12 for housing, about twenty times as responsive.

3 · Share of the budget

Salt doubles in price and few people notice, because it was 40¢. Rent rises 10% and the rest of the budget has to change. Demand for cheap things tends to be inelastic because the price barely matters to the budget.

4 · Time

Gasoline jumps this week and you still drive to work. Give it two years and you have moved, changed cars, or changed jobs. Demand is more elastic in the long run, because adjusting takes time.

Sort these — commit before you click

Elastic or inelastic? (a) insulin  ·  (b) one particular brand of jeans  ·  (c) gasoline next week  ·  (d) gasoline over the next five years

(a) Inelastic — a necessity with no substitute. (b) Elastic — a shelf full of substitutes. (c) Inelastic — no time to adjust. (d) Elastic — the same good, once people have had time to change how they live. Compare (c) and (d): same product, opposite answer, and the only difference is the time allowed to adjust.

Substitutes, necessity, budget share, time. Name which one applies and you can predict the answer.
Instructor notes

Items (c) and (d) are the pair worth the discussion; do not resolve them until somebody in the room says “it is the same good.”

WATCH FORDay 2

Six common errors this week, and the correction for each

Common Module 4 errors and the correction for each
The trapWhy it is wrongWhat to do instead
“The price went up, so demand fell.” Confuses a movement along demand with a shift of it — the Week 2 error, now with a second curve on the graph. Name the variable that changed first. If it was this good's own price, nothing shifted.
Shifting both curves for a single event. One event changes one thing. Shifting both usually means you shifted the consequence as well as the cause. Do step 2 of the four-step process out loud: buyers, or sellers?
Measuring a shortage vertically. A shortage is a gap between two quantities at one price, so it is measured along a horizontal line. Put your finger on the stated price, run it across, and read the distance between the curves.
Assuming every price control does something. A ceiling above equilibrium, or a floor below it, changes nothing at all. Ask “does it bind?” before anything else.
Calling a transfer a loss. Money moving from sellers to buyers is not destroyed — someone has it. Deadweight loss is value that never comes into existence. Ask “does somebody end up holding this dollar?” If yes, it is a transfer.
Judging elasticity by how steep the curve looks. Steepness depends on the units you happened to choose for the axes. Find the two percentages and compare them. Bigger quantity change → elastic.

And one habit: answer with units. “Price rises to $1.60 per gallon and quantity falls to 550 million gallons” is an answer. “It goes up” is not.
