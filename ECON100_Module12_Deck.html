MONEYDay 1

What money does: three functions

Without money, people trade goods for goods. That is barter. A plumber who wants bread must find a baker who needs a plumber at the same time. Economists call that a double coincidence of wants. It makes most trades slow or impossible.

Medium of exchange

Money is accepted as payment for goods and services. The plumber is paid in dollars and can spend the dollars at any bakery.

Store of value

Money keeps its buying power from today until a later day. A paycheck can be spent next month. A bushel of tomatoes spoils.

Unit of account

Prices are stated in money, so values are easy to compare. A car priced at $8,400 costs $700 less than a car priced at $9,100.

Money is whatever people generally accept in payment for goods and services and in repayment of debts.

Checkpoint — commit before you reveal

A student saves $40 a week in a checking account to pay for a trip next summer. Which function of money does the account serve?

Store of value. The student is holding buying power until next summer. When the student pays for the trip, the same dollars serve as a medium of exchange.

Money is a medium of exchange, a store of value, and a unit of account.
Instructor notes

OpenStax §27.1 also names a fourth function, a standard of deferred payment. Mandel calls the unit of account a standard of value. The course uses the three names above.

MONEYDay 1

Commodity money and fiat money

Commodity money

Commodity money is a good that has value of its own. Gold and silver coins are examples. In World War II prison camps, prisoners used cigarettes as money. A paper note that can be exchanged for a fixed amount of gold is commodity-backed money.

Fiat money

Fiat money has no value of its own as a good. It cannot be exchanged for gold. It has value because the government declares it legal tender and because people expect others to accept it. A $20 bill is fiat money.

How the U.S. dollar became fiat money
YearWhat changed
1933–1934Americans could no longer exchange dollars for gold. Foreign governments still could, at $35 an ounce.
Aug 15, 1971President Nixon stopped foreign governments from exchanging dollars for gold. Since then, no one has been able to exchange dollars for gold at a fixed price.
Checkpoint — commit before you reveal

A $20 bill costs the government only a few cents to print. It cannot be exchanged for gold. Why does a store accept it for $20 of goods?

Because the store expects others to accept it. The store can use the same bill to pay its workers and its suppliers. The law also requires that the bill be accepted to pay debts. If people stopped expecting others to accept dollars, the dollar would lose its value as money. Years of very high inflation could cause that.

Fiat money has value because people expect others to accept it.
Instructor notes

The cigarette example is R. A. Radford, “The Economic Organisation of a P.O.W. Camp,” Economica (1945). The gold dates: the 1933 orders and the Gold Reserve Act of 1934; the August 15, 1971 address.

MONEYDay 1

Measuring money: M1 and M2

The Federal Reserve measures the money supply in two ways. M1 is currency held by the public plus checking and savings deposits. M2 is M1 plus small time deposits, such as certificates of deposit, and retail money market funds.

Most money is deposits

In September 2026, M1 was $20.1 trillion. Currency was $2.4 trillion of that, or about 12 percent. Deposits were most of the rest. So most money today is a number in a bank’s records, not paper.

A credit card is not money

A credit card arranges a loan from the card company. The card company pays the store, and you repay the card company later from your checking account. So the card is not money. It is an agreement to borrow. A debit card is not money either. It moves money that is already in your checking account.

Checkpoint — commit before you reveal

You move $500 from your checking account into a one-year certificate of deposit. What happens to M1 and to M2?

M1 falls by $500, and M2 does not change. The checking deposit is in M1. The certificate of deposit is in M2 but not in M1. M2 counts both, so M2 stays the same.

M1 is currency plus checking and savings deposits. M2 adds small time deposits and money market funds.
Instructor notes

Since May 2020, M1 includes savings deposits, so M1 jumped that month by definition. OpenStax §27.2 explains the change. Data: Federal Reserve H.6 through FRED, series CURRSL, M1SL, and M2SL, September 2026.

HISTORYDay 1

History: the First and Second Banks of the United States, 1791–1836

After the Revolutionary War, the new country had war debts and many kinds of state money. Treasury Secretary Alexander Hamilton wanted one national bank. The bank would hold the government’s money, lend to the government, and give the country a sound currency.

The First Bank, 1791–1811

Congress chartered the First Bank of the United States in 1791 for 20 years. Thomas Jefferson and others opposed it. They argued that the Constitution did not allow a national bank and that the bank gave too much power to a few financiers. In 1811, Congress did not renew the charter.

The Second Bank, 1816–1836

The War of 1812 left the government with new debts and an unstable currency. So in 1816, Congress chartered the Second Bank of the United States, again for 20 years. President Andrew Jackson vetoed its renewal in 1832, and the charter ended in 1836.

A central bank is the bank for the government and for other banks. It holds their reserves, lends to them, and works to keep the money and banking system stable. The First and Second Banks did some of this work. The United States then had no central bank from 1836 to 1913.

The country tried a national bank twice. Both times, the charter was not renewed.
Instructor notes

The disagreement over the two banks is a disagreement over power: a strong national institution against state banks and local control. It returns with the Federal Reserve in 1913. Source: Federal Reserve History essays on the First and Second Banks.

HISTORYDay 1

History: state bank notes, national banks, and panics, 1836–1913

State bank notes, 1836–1863

Banks chartered by the states printed their own paper money, called bank notes. Thousands of different notes were in use. A note from a distant or doubtful bank was often accepted for less than its face value. Counterfeits were common.

National banks, from 1863

During the Civil War, the National Banking Acts created national banks. A new federal office, the Comptroller of the Currency, chartered and examined them. National bank notes all looked the same and were backed by U.S. government bonds. In 1865, Congress passed a 10 percent tax on state bank notes, and most state notes went out of use.

Panics

The national banking system still had no lender for banks in trouble. Banking panics struck in 1873, 1893, and 1907. In the Panic of 1907, the banker J. P. Morgan organized other bankers to lend to banks whose depositors were rushing to withdraw.

Checkpoint — commit before you reveal

In 1850, a shopkeeper is handed a $5 note from a bank in another state. Why might the shopkeeper accept it only as $4.50?

Because the note might not be paid in full. The shopkeeper cannot easily check whether the distant bank is sound or whether the note is counterfeit. The lower value covers that risk. After 1863, national bank notes backed by U.S. bonds removed most of that risk.

The National Banking Acts gave the country one currency. They did not give it a lender for banks in a panic.
Instructor notes

Circulation of state bank notes fell from $143 million in 1865 to $4 million by 1867 (Federal Reserve History, “National Banking Acts of 1863 and 1864”). The $4.50 is an illustration, not a quoted rate.

HISTORYDay 1

History: the Federal Reserve, 1913

After the Panic of 1907, Congress studied central banks in other countries. On December 23, 1913, President Woodrow Wilson signed the Federal Reserve Act. The act created the Federal Reserve, or the Fed, the country’s third central bank.

How the Fed is organized

The Fed has a Board of Governors in Washington, D.C. Its 7 members are appointed by the President and confirmed by the Senate for 14-year terms. The Fed also has 12 regional Federal Reserve Banks. Spreading the Fed across 12 regions answered an old worry from 1791 and 1832: that one bank in one city would hold too much power.

What it was created to do

The Fed holds banks’ reserves and supplies the currency. It can also lend to sound banks when depositors rush to withdraw. A central bank that lends in a panic is called a lender of last resort.

The Federal Reserve, created in 1913, is the bank for banks and the lender of last resort.
Instructor notes

Week 8 covered the bank failures of 1930–1933, when the Fed did not stop the panics. Day 2 returns to that story with deposit insurance. Week 13 covers the Fed’s monetary policy tools.

BANKSDay 1

What a bank does, and how to read its T-account

A bank is a financial intermediary. It takes deposits from savers and lends to borrowers. So a saver does not have to find a borrower, and a borrower does not have to find a saver.

Reading a T-account

A T-account lists a bank’s assets on the left and its liabilities on the right. Assets are what the bank owns, such as reserves and loans. Liabilities are what the bank owes, such as deposits. Customers can withdraw their deposits.

In Lakeland, Ana deposits $1,000 of cash in Ashford Bank. The bank now owes Ana $1,000, so deposits rise by $1,000. The cash becomes the bank’s reserves. Reserves are the cash in the bank’s vault plus its account at the Federal Reserve.

A real bank also has bank capital. Bank capital is the owners’ money in the bank: assets minus liabilities. If the bank loses money, bank capital takes the loss before depositors lose anything. The examples in this deck leave bank capital out to keep the T-accounts short.

A deposit is a liability of the bank. The cash it brings in is an asset, the bank’s reserves.
Instructor notes

Lakeland is the Week 11 economy, reused so that students meet one new idea at a time. Ashford, Birch, and Cedar Banks carry the Day 2 build.

BANKSDay 2

Reserves and the reserve ratio

On a normal day, only a few depositors withdraw their money. So a bank keeps part of its deposits as reserves and lends the rest. This system is called fractional reserve banking.

The reserve ratio is the share of deposits that a bank keeps as reserves. Reserve ratio = reserves / deposits.

The required ratio is 0 percent

Before 2020, the Federal Reserve required banks to keep up to 10 percent of their checking deposits as reserves. On March 26, 2020, the Fed lowered the required ratio to 0 percent. It is still 0 percent. Banks still keep reserves, because every day they must pay withdrawals and make payments to other banks.

This week’s example

In Lakeland, each bank chooses to keep 20 percent of each deposit as reserves. It lends the other 80 percent. Ashford Bank keeps 20 percent of Ana’s $1,000 deposit, which is $1,000 × 0.20 = $200. It lends $1,000 − $200 = $800.

A bank keeps part of each deposit as reserves and lends the rest.
Instructor notes

OpenStax §27.4 is written with a required ratio of 10 percent; §28.3 records the March 2020 change. The deck teaches the ratio the bank chooses, with the one-step formula for the multiplier.

MONEY CREATIONDay 2

Build #1: a deposit, a loan, and a re-deposit

Follow Ana’s $1,000 through three of Lakeland’s banks. Each bank keeps 20 percent of each deposit as reserves and lends the rest.

  1. The deposit. Ana deposits $1,000 of cash in Ashford Bank. Deposits rise by $1,000, and reserves rise by $1,000. The money supply has not changed yet, because $1,000 of cash became a $1,000 deposit.
  2. The loan. Ashford Bank keeps $200 as reserves. It lends $800 to Ben, a Lakeland business owner, by adding $800 to his checking account. Ana still has her $1,000 deposit, and Ben now has $800 to spend. So the loan created $800 of new money. When Ben pays for equipment, his $800 leaves Ashford Bank. Ashford is left with $200 of reserves and the $800 loan.
  3. The re-deposit. Ben spends the $800 on equipment, and the seller deposits it in Birch Bank. Birch Bank keeps 20 percent, which is $160, and lends $640.
  4. The next round. The $640 is spent and deposited in Cedar Bank. Cedar Bank keeps $128 and lends $512. In three rounds, the loans created $800 + $640 + $512 = $1,952 of new money. Each round is smaller, because each bank keeps 20 percent.
Step 1 of 4

Why a loan creates money. A bank makes a loan by adding to the borrower’s deposit. The first depositor still has her deposit, and the borrower has a new one. Both deposits can be spent, so the money supply is larger.

When a bank lends, it creates a new deposit. That deposit is new money.
Instructor notes

The common error is to say the bank “lends out Ana’s money,” so no money is created. Ana’s deposit is still hers and still spendable. The optional money-creation demonstration acts out this build with play money.

MONEY CREATIONDay 2

The money multiplier: the limit on new deposits

The rounds continue. The $512 is deposited, and the next bank keeps $102.40 and lends $409.60. Each round is 80 percent of the round before. Total deposits rise toward a limit but never pass it.

What the bars count

Each bar counts Ana’s deposit plus the deposits that sellers make at the next bank. A loan shows up in a bar only after it is spent and deposited. After round 3, total deposits are $2,440, and Cedar Bank’s $512 loan is not yet in the bar. So $2,440 − $1,000 = $1,440, which is less than the $1,952 that the three loans created. To find the new money after a few rounds, add the loans. Subtract the first deposit from total deposits only at the limit.

The money multiplier is the largest amount of deposits that the banking system can create from each $1 of new reserves. Money multiplier = 1 / reserve ratio.

Lakeland’s numbers

The reserve ratio is 0.20, so the money multiplier is 1 / 0.20 = 5. Total deposits can reach $1,000 × 5 = $5,000. Ana’s $1,000 was already money, as cash. So the loans can create up to $5,000 − $1,000 = $4,000 of new money.

Checkpoint — commit before you reveal

Suppose Lakeland’s banks grow cautious and keep 50 percent of each deposit. What is the money multiplier, and how far can total deposits grow from Ana’s $1,000?

The multiplier is 2, and total deposits can reach $2,000. The multiplier is 1 / 0.50 = 2, and $1,000 × 2 = $2,000. When banks keep more of each deposit, they lend less. So less new money is created.

A lower reserve ratio means a larger money multiplier and more money created by loans.
Instructor notes

The multiplier is a one-step formula, used after the chain has shown what it means. The figure’s first three bars are the build’s numbers: $1,000, $1,800, and $2,440.

MONEY CREATIONDay 2

Why real money creation is smaller than the limit

The multiplier gives the most money that the banking system can create. It assumes that every dollar lent is spent and deposited again. It also assumes that every bank lends all it can. In a real economy, three things keep money creation below the limit.

People hold cash

Sometimes a borrower or a seller keeps part of a payment as cash. That cash is not deposited, so the next bank has less to lend.

Banks keep extra reserves

Banks may worry about withdrawals or about borrowers who might not repay. Then they keep more reserves than they planned and lend less.

Borrowers borrow less

A bank can lend only to people and firms who want to borrow. In a recession, fewer businesses want loans.

The required reserve ratio is 0 percent, so the law does not fix the multiplier. The multiplier depends on the reserves that banks choose to keep and the cash that people choose to hold. Both change as the economy changes.

The money multiplier gives a limit. Real money creation is usually smaller.
Instructor notes

After 2008, banks held large reserves, and the money multiplier fell well below the textbook value. Students do not need the data; the idea is that the multiplier changes.

REGULATIONDay 2

Bank runs: why a sound bank can fail

After Build #1, Ashford Bank has $200 of reserves and $1,000 of deposits. The other $800 is in a loan that will be repaid over several years. If Ana asks for all $1,000 today, the bank cannot pay her from its reserves.

A bank run happens when many depositors try to withdraw their money at once because they fear the bank will fail. Each depositor has a reason to be first in line, because the first depositors are the ones who get paid.

The 1930s

In Week 8, we covered the bank failures of the Great Depression. From 1930 through 1933, about 9,000 banks suspended operations, which means they closed their doors. A run on one bank made depositors at other banks fear for their money. So the runs spread.

The answer in 1933

The Banking Act of 1933 created the Federal Deposit Insurance Corporation (FDIC). Starting January 1, 1934, the FDIC insured each depositor’s deposits up to $2,500. An insured depositor has no reason to rush to withdraw.

Checkpoint — commit before you reveal

A rumor says a bank lost money on its loans. The rumor is false. Why can the bank still fail?

Because its reserves cover only part of its deposits. If enough depositors believe the rumor and withdraw at once, the bank runs out of reserves. It cannot collect on its loans fast enough to pay them. So the fear alone can make the bank fail, even though its loans are sound.

Because banks keep only part of their deposits as reserves, a run can close a sound bank.
Instructor notes

The 9,000 figure is the Week 8 number. A lender of last resort can lend reserves to a sound bank during a run; the Fed did not do enough of that in 1930–1933.

REGULATIONDay 2

How U.S. banking is organized and regulated

The United States has a dual banking system. A bank can be chartered by the federal government, as a national bank, or by a state. Several agencies supervise banks, and each agency has its own job.

Deposit insurance

The FDIC insures deposits up to $250,000 per depositor, per insured bank, for each account ownership category. Congress raised the limit to $250,000 in 2008 and made it permanent in 2010.

Capital and examinations

Supervisors require banks to hold enough bank capital to cover losses. Examiners check each bank’s loans and risks.

Lender of last resort

The Federal Reserve can lend reserves to a sound bank that faces large withdrawals.

Checkpoint — commit before you reveal

Jo has $310,000 in a single checking account at one bank, and the bank fails. How much of Jo’s deposit is insured, and how much is not?

$250,000 is insured, and $60,000 is not. The limit is $250,000 per depositor at one bank. So $310,000 − $250,000 = $60,000 is uninsured. Jo may get back part of the $60,000 later, when the failed bank’s assets are sold.

Deposit insurance, capital rules, examinations, and a lender of last resort make bank runs less likely.
Instructor notes

Deposit insurance creates a cost of its own: insured depositors have less reason to watch the risks their bank takes. That tradeoff is the subject of the positive-or-normative checkpoint on the next slide.

CASEDay 2

Case: Silicon Valley Bank, March 2023

Silicon Valley Bank was in Santa Clara, California. At the end of 2022, it had $209 billion of assets. Most of its depositors were technology companies with large accounts. About 88 percent of its deposits were above the $250,000 insurance limit.

Why it failed

The bank had put much of its deposits into long-term bonds that paid low interest rates. When interest rates rose in 2022, new bonds paid higher interest. Buyers would then pay less for the bank’s older, lower-paying bonds, so those bonds lost value. The bank sold some of its bonds at a loss. Uninsured depositors then feared that they would not be paid in full, and they rushed to withdraw. Because depositors could withdraw from their phones, the run took hours instead of days.

What regulators did

On March 12, 2023, the FDIC, the Federal Reserve, and the Treasury announced that every depositor would be paid in full. That included the uninsured depositors. The goal was to stop runs at other banks. A special fee on banks paid the cost, not taxes.

Checkpoint — positive or normative?

Statement A: “About 88 percent of Silicon Valley Bank’s deposits were uninsured at the end of 2022.” Statement B: “Regulators should protect every depositor in a failed bank, whatever the size of the deposit.” Which statement is positive, and which is normative?

A is positive, and B is normative. Statement A can be checked against the bank’s records. Statement B is a value judgment. Protecting every depositor can stop runs. It can also give large depositors less reason to watch the risks their bank takes. Evidence can measure both effects, but deciding which matters more is a value judgment.

Silicon Valley Bank failed in a run by uninsured depositors, two days after it reported a loss on its bonds.
Instructor notes

Sources: FDIC Chairman Gruenberg’s testimony, March 28, 2023 (88 percent uninsured); Vice Chair Barr’s testimony and the Federal Reserve review of April 28, 2023 ($1.8 billion loss on $21 billion of securities, $42 billion withdrawn on March 9, $100 billion more expected March 10); the joint statement of March 12, 2023. The fee is the FDIC special assessment on large banks.

WATCH FORWrap-up

Six common errors this week

Common Module 12 errors and the correction for each
The errorWhy it is wrongWhat to do instead
Calling a credit card money. A credit card arranges a loan. The card company pays the store, and you repay the loan later. Count the checking deposit that pays the bill, not the card.
Putting deposits on the asset side of a bank’s T-account. A deposit is money the bank owes to the depositor. Put deposits under liabilities, and reserves and loans under assets.
Saying a loan creates no money because the bank “lends out” a deposit. The first depositor still has her deposit, and the borrower has a new one. Count the loan as new money.
Multiplying by the reserve ratio instead of dividing 1 by it. The money multiplier is 1 / reserve ratio, which is larger than 1. With a ratio of 0.20, the multiplier is 1 / 0.20 = 5.
Counting the first deposit as new money. The first $1,000 was money already, as cash. Add up the loans. At the limit, new money from loans = total deposits − the first deposit.
Saying the law requires banks to keep 10 percent of deposits. The required ratio has been 0 percent since March 26, 2020. Use the ratio that the banks choose to keep.

Before you fill in a T-account, ask what the bank owns and what it owes. Reserves and loans are owned. Deposits are owed.

📝 Exam 3 — Thursday, Nov 19

Exam 3 covers Weeks 10–12. It is in class, on paper. From this week, it covers the functions of money, M1 and M2, the history of American banking, T-accounts and the money multiplier, and deposit insurance.

The main idea of Module 12. Money is a medium of exchange, a store of value, and a unit of account. The dollar is fiat money. Most of the money supply is bank deposits. A bank keeps part of each deposit as reserves and lends the rest. Each loan creates a new deposit, so the banking system creates money. The money multiplier, 1 / reserve ratio, gives the limit. Because banks keep only part of their deposits as reserves, a run can make a bank fail. The country responded step by step: national banks in 1863, the Federal Reserve in 1913, and deposit insurance in 1933. In 2023, Silicon Valley Bank showed that runs can still happen when most deposits are uninsured.

Banks create most of the money people spend. That is why banks are insured and supervised.
