What this week covers

This week, we ask what competition does in a market. We follow one sweetcorn farm that cannot choose its own price. When the farm makes an economic profit, other farms have a reason to start growing sweetcorn, and their entry lowers the price until the economic profit is gone. No one plans that result. In Part 2, we see that it produces two kinds of efficiency.

Part 1: one farm

We start with the four conditions that make a market perfectly competitive. Then we build the farm's costs, find how many crates it grows at a given price, and decide when it should stop growing sweetcorn for a season.

Part 2: the whole market over time

We follow the market as new farms enter. Then we see why the price stops falling where it does, and we state what the result achieves and what it does not.

VI · Wk 6Part 1

The four characteristics of a perfectly competitive market

A market is perfectly competitive when four conditions hold. When they hold, we can predict what each firm will do and where the market price will go.

The four characteristics of a perfectly competitive market and why each one is on the list
CharacteristicWhat it meansWhy it matters
Many sellersEach seller supplies a small share of the market.One seller cannot change the market price by growing more or less.
An identical productBuyers cannot tell one seller's product from another's.No buyer will pay one seller more than the going price.
Free entry and exitA new firm can start selling, and an existing firm can stop, without a large barrier.The long-run result in Part 2 depends on this condition.
Full information about pricesBuyers and sellers know the going price.A seller cannot charge more without buyers noticing.
Checkpoint — which markets come close?

Very few real markets meet all four conditions. Why study a market that is so rare?

We study it because it is the benchmark for judging real markets. Markets for wheat, corn, eggs, and many other farm products come close to the four conditions. A coffee shop or a phone maker does not.

Perfect competition shows what competition does when all four conditions hold. In Week 7, we compare real markets with this benchmark to see what changes when a condition fails.

Watch for: reading “perfect” as “best.” In this term, “perfect” means that all four conditions hold. Whether the result is good for society is a separate question, and we take it up at the end of Part 2.

Instructor notes

Take the conditions one at a time and ask for a market that fails each one. Students name examples quickly: a phone, a hair salon, a campus parking permit.

The watch-for on “perfect” is worth saying out loud.

VI · Wk 6Part 1

The market sets the price, and the farm takes it

Our example is a sweetcorn farm, one of 200 farms that sell the same sweetcorn by the crate. The market demand curve slopes down, as it has since Week 2. But the demand curve that one farm faces is flat. The two graphs do not conflict, because they measure different quantities.

Why the farm's demand is flat

One farm grows a few crates a week in a market of 1,400 crates. Whether it grows 1 more crate or 5 more, the market price does not change by a noticeable amount.

What follows

Each additional crate the farm sells brings in exactly $24. The extra revenue from one more unit is called marginal revenue. For this farm, marginal revenue equals the price at every quantity.

A price taker is a firm that must accept the market price, because nothing it does can change that price. Every firm in a perfectly competitive market is a price taker, and for a price taker, marginal revenue = price.

Instructor notes

Put both graphs up and ask why one demand curve slopes down and the other is flat. The answer is the scale of the horizontal axes: the whole market against one farm.

Board MR = P and leave it up for the rest of the class.

VI · Wk 6Part 1

The farm's costs: fixed, variable, and marginal

The farm cannot choose its price, so the only thing it chooses is how many crates to grow. To make that choice, it needs its costs. These costs include the implicit costs from Week 3, such as the farmer's own time.

Fixed cost: $26 a week

The farm leases its field for $26 a week. It owes the lease whether it grows 0 crates or 8. A fixed cost is a cost that does not change with the quantity produced.

Variable cost: seed, water, and labor

Growing more crates takes more seed, more water, and more hours of work, including the farmer’s own hours, valued at what she could earn elsewhere. A variable cost is a cost that rises with the quantity produced. Total cost is fixed cost plus variable cost.

VI · Wk 6Part 1

Why marginal cost eventually rises

The marginal cost row falls from $15 to $11 and then rises to $32. Both movements come from how the work is done on a field of fixed size.

Crates 1 to 3: marginal cost falls

With one worker, one person does every job. When a second worker joins, each worker can focus on fewer tasks, so each crate takes less work. This is the division of labor that Adam Smith described in 1776 in his pin factory, which we met in Week 1.

Crates 4 to 8: marginal cost rises

The field does not grow when workers are added. After a point, each added worker has less room and less soil to work with, so each worker adds fewer crates than the one before. Each additional crate then takes more hours of work, so the marginal cost of each additional crate is higher.

Diminishing marginal returns means that when one input is fixed, adding more of another input eventually adds less and less output. Here the field is the fixed input, and labor is the input being added.

Watch for: explaining diminishing marginal returns as workers getting tired or lazy. The eighth worker is as able as the third. Output per added worker falls because the field is fixed.

Marginal cost rises because the field is fixed, and each added worker adds fewer crates than the one before.
Instructor notes

Say the watch-for, then say its negation: “The eighth worker is not worse than the third.” The misconception survives the positive statement unless the negation is said as well.

The pin factory is a one-sentence callback. Smith's point was about the gain from dividing tasks, which is why marginal cost falls at first.

VI · Wk 6Part 1

Where marginal cost crosses average total cost

Here are the three cost curves from the cost table, drawn on one graph. Marginal cost crosses average total cost at exactly one point: 6 crates and $18 per crate.

Marginal cost crosses average total cost at the lowest point of average total cost. An average works the same way as a course grade. A score above your average raises your average, and a score below it lowers your average. While the cost of the next crate is below the average, the average falls. Once the cost of the next crate is above the average, the average rises.

The cost table shows this. At 4 crates, average total cost is $19.00, and the 5th crate costs $14, so the average falls to $18.00. The 6th crate costs $18, the same as the average, so the average stays at $18.00. The 7th crate costs $24, so the average rises to $18.86.

Instructor notes

Work the grade example first: ask for a score that would raise an 80% average, then one that would lower it. Then apply it to the 6-crate row, where the 6th crate costs $18 and the average is $18.

The same decision, from the table

You do not need the graph to find the profit-maximizing output. The graph shows the marginal cost column of the cost table, so the column gives the same answer.

Checkpoint — use only the table

The price is $24. Using the marginal cost column, how many crates should the farm grow, and how do you know where to stop?

7 crates. Read down the marginal cost column: $15, $12, $11, $12, $14, $18, $24. The marginal cost of each of those crates is no more than the $24 it sells for, so each one is worth growing. The marginal cost of the 8th crate is $32, which is more than $24, so the farm stops at 7.

The table and the graph give the same answer, and either one is a complete method.

Watch for: choosing the output where average total cost is lowest. That is 6 crates, and it is the wrong answer at $24. The marginal cost of the 7th crate is $24, and the crate sells for $24, so growing it does not lower profit. The lowest cost per crate and the highest profit are different questions.

The output decision uses marginal cost. The averages tell you whether the farm is making a profit or a loss.
Instructor notes

Many students find the table easier than the graph, and both are complete answers. After the checkpoint, ask why a farm would accept a higher average cost at 7 crates. The answer is that the 7th crate still adds as much to revenue as to cost.

VI · Wk 6Part 1

A loss is not always a reason to stop

Here is the same farm at four market prices. The farm loses money at two of them. At one of those two prices it should keep growing, and at the other it should leave the field empty for the week.

Checkpoint — commit first

At $14, the farm loses $20 a week and keeps growing sweetcorn. Why does that make sense?

Because leaving the field empty loses $26. The farm owes the $26 lease either way. At $14, revenue of $70 covers the $64 of variable cost and puts $6 toward the lease. Leaving the field empty covers none of the lease.

The marginal cost of the 1st crate is $15, which is more than $14. But the marginal costs of crates 2 through 5 are below $14, so the 5 crates together cover their $64 of variable cost. That is why the second step of the rule compares revenue with variable cost, not one crate’s marginal cost with the price.

At $12, growing sweetcorn loses at least $28. At 4 crates, for example, revenue of $48 does not cover the $50 of variable cost. Leaving the field empty loses only the $26 lease, so the farm stops for the week.

This is the sunk-cost idea from Week 3. The lease is owed whatever the farm decides, so it cannot change which choice is better.

The complete rule has two steps. First, find the largest quantity at which marginal cost is no more than the price. Second, check whether revenue at that quantity covers variable cost. If it does not, the firm produces nothing for now.

The shut-down rule: a firm keeps producing as long as the price covers its average variable cost. When the price falls below the lowest average variable cost, the firm shuts down for now. For this farm, the shut-down price is $12.50, the lowest AVC in the cost table.

Watch for: “The farm is losing money, so it should shut down.” A firm shuts down only when the price is below its average variable cost. Some businesses operate at a loss for a season, because operating loses less than shutting down.

Instructor notes

At $14, expect many students to say “shut down.” The sunk-cost callback is what changes minds. The optional pair workshop sheet covers this row.

Ask for a real example: a ski resort in a year with little snow, or a beach restaurant in January.

VI · Wk 6Part 1

The short run and the long run

The shut-down rule depends on the lease being owed no matter what. That is true only for a while, and economists name the two periods by what can change in each.

⏱️ The short run

The short run is a period in which at least one input is fixed. Our farm has signed its lease, so it cannot change the size of its field. It can only change how much seed, water, and labor it uses.

In the short run, the number of farms in the market is also fixed.

🔄 The long run

The long run is a period long enough for every input to change. The lease ends, so the farmer can rent a larger field or stop farming. New farmers can rent land and start growing sweetcorn.

In the long run, firms can enter the market or exit it.

Watch for: treating the short run as a fixed number of months. For a food truck, the long run may be a few weeks. For a power plant, it may be many years. The difference is whether every input can change.

In the short run, a firm decides how much to produce. In the long run, firms also decide whether to be in the market at all.
Instructor notes

This slide closes Part 1 and sets up Part 2. Ask which inputs are fixed for a campus coffee cart and for an airline.

PART 2Part 2

Part 2: what happens to the $36 economic profit?

At $24 a crate, our farm makes $36 a week in economic profit. Every farm in the market has the same costs, so every farm makes that profit. Farmers who grow other crops can see the price of sweetcorn, and nothing stops them from renting a field and growing sweetcorn.

Predict first

What happens over the next few seasons, and when does it stop? Write one or two sentences before you read on.

New farms enter, and the price falls. Each new farm adds to the quantity supplied at every price, so the market supply curve shifts to the right and the market price falls. As the price falls, each farm’s economic profit shrinks.

Entry stops when no economic profit is left to give another farm a reason to enter. The next two slides show where that happens.

Economic profit is a reason for new firms to enter a market.

VI · Wk 6Part 2

Why entry stops at $18

What zero economic profit means

At $18, the farm grows 6 crates, and revenue of $108 equals total cost of $108. Economic profit is zero. The farmer still earns an income, because total cost includes her implicit costs, such as the value of her own time. Zero economic profit means that she earns exactly what she could earn in her next best alternative.

Why the price stops at $18

Above $18, farms make an economic profit, and new farms have a reason to enter. Below $18, farms make a loss, and some farms have a reason to exit when their leases end. Exit shifts supply to the left and raises the price. Only at $18 does no farm have a reason to enter or exit.

In long-run equilibrium, the price equals the lowest average total cost, and each firm makes zero economic profit. Entry and exit stop, because no firm has a reason to enter or leave.

Watch for: “zero economic profit means the farm is failing.” The farm covers every cost, including the owner's time. Zero economic profit is the normal long-run position of a firm in a competitive market.

Instructor notes

Expect the question “so the farmer works for free?” The answer is no: her wage is inside the cost curve. Connect it to the implicit costs from the opening brain dump.

Ask what happens if the price falls to $16 because too many farms entered. Students can work the exit story themselves.

VI · Wk 6Part 2

What the long-run price achieves

In long-run equilibrium, the price of $18 is equal to two different costs. Each equality describes a different kind of efficiency.

🎯 Price = marginal cost

The last crate grown costs $18 to produce, and some buyer is willing to pay $18 for it. Every crate that a buyer values at more than its cost gets grown. No crate gets grown if it costs more than buyers value it.

This is allocative efficiency: the market produces the quantity at which the value of the last unit to buyers equals its cost.

⚙️ Price = lowest average total cost

Each farm grows 6 crates, and its average total cost there is $18, the lowest it can reach. A farm with a higher cost per crate would make a loss at $18.

This is productive efficiency: each unit is produced at the lowest possible average cost.

Where this idea came from. In 1776, Adam Smith wrote that an individual who “intends only his own gain” is “led by an invisible hand to promote an end which was no part of his intention.” Our farmers are an example. Each farmer tried only to earn a profit, and the result was sweetcorn produced at the lowest cost and sold at that cost. Smith added “as in many other cases,” which is a narrower claim than “in every case.”

Allocative efficiency is about how much gets produced. Productive efficiency is about how cheaply it gets produced.
Instructor notes

Keep the two kinds of efficiency apart on the board: allocative answers “how much?” and productive answers “how cheaply?” Students often merge them.

Smith used the phrase once in The Wealth of Nations and qualified it in the same sentence. That is a fact about the source worth saying.

VI · Wk 6Part 2

What the efficiency result does not say

The efficiency result is a positive claim about what a competitive market produces. It has three limits, and each one is part of standard economics.

It says nothing about who gets the goods

Demand counts willingness to pay, which depends on income. A person who wants sweetcorn but cannot pay for it does not appear on the demand curve. Efficiency and fairness are different questions.

It assumes the price includes every cost

If growing sweetcorn pollutes a river and no farm pays for the damage, the market price leaves out a real cost. Then the quantity the market produces is not the efficient quantity. We take this up in Week 8.

It needs all four conditions

The result depends on many sellers, an identical product, free entry and exit, and full information about prices. In Week 7, we look at markets in which some of these conditions fail.

Checkpoint — positive or normative?

Someone says: “Competitive markets produce the efficient quantity, so the government should stay out of them.” Which part of that statement is positive, and which part is normative?

The first part is positive, and the second part is normative. “Competitive markets produce the efficient quantity” is a positive claim. Evidence can test it. The model in this module predicts it when the four conditions hold and the price includes every cost.

“The government should stay out of them” is a normative claim. To reach it, a person must also judge that efficiency matters more than who gets the goods, and must check that the conditions hold in the market being discussed. The model does not make either judgment.

Instructor notes

Present the limits as standard economics, not as objections to markets. Students who think they are being sold a position stop listening, and so do students who think markets are being attacked.

Six common errors this week

The errorWhy it is wrongWhat to do instead
“Perfect competition means the best kind of market.” “Perfect” means that all four conditions hold. Whether the outcome is good is a separate question. List the four conditions when you use the term.

The main idea of Module 6. In a market with many sellers, an identical product, and free entry, economic profit does not last. Economic profit gives new firms a reason to enter, entry lowers the price, and the price falls until the economic profit is gone. The result is goods produced at the lowest average cost and sold at that cost.

In a competitive market, entry and exit move the price to the lowest average total cost, and no one plans that result.
