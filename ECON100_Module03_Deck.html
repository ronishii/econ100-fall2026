Predict first

This week is about cost and about supply. Before you read another word: what do you think the connection is? Why would a week that starts with opportunity cost end with a supply curve? One sentence.

A supply curve is a cost curve. Suppose a neighbor offers to pay you to mow a lawn on Saturday. The least you would take is whatever that Saturday afternoon is worth to you — a shift at work, a nap, time with friends. §2.1Day 1

Cost is not the same thing as cash

In ordinary English, “what did it cost?” means “how much money did you hand over?” In economics that is only ever part of the answer — and often the smaller part.

💰 EXPLICIT cost

Money that actually leaves your hand. It has a receipt. Tuition, gas, rent, the price of the ticket.

Easy to see, easy to add up, and never the whole story.

⌛ IMPLICIT cost

Value you give up without any money changing hands. The wages you did not earn because you were in class. The hour you did not spend on something else.

No receipt is ever issued for an implicit cost. That is exactly why people miss them.

Opportunity cost = explicit cost + implicit cost. Week 1 defined opportunity cost as “the value of the next best alternative.” This is the same definition, split into the part with a receipt and the part without.

⚠️ Watch out for: treating “free” as “costless.” A free concert on the quad still costs you the two hours. Zero explicit cost is not zero opportunity cost.

Every choice has a price tag with two lines on it. Only one of them has a dollar sign printed on it already.
Instructor notes

A live example for each card before showing them: “what did coming to campus this morning cost you?” Students give gas and parking (explicit) fast; the implicit half usually needs a prompt — “what else could that ninety minutes have been?”

[Board] opportunity cost = explicit + implicit is worth leaving up. Everything in Part 1 hangs off it, and HW3a is graded against it.

§2.1Day 1

The question professional economists get wrong

Predict first · commit before you click

You won a free ticket to see Artist A tonight. It cannot be resold. Artist B is playing the same night; a B ticket costs $40, and seeing B is worth $50 to you. There are no other costs.

What is your opportunity cost of seeing Artist A?  (a) $0   (b) $10   (c) $40   (d) $50

(b) $10. Your next best alternative is seeing B. Going to B would have been worth $50 to you, but you would have had to pay $40 for it — so the net value you give up by not going is $50 − $40 = $10.

Why this is famous: when this exact question was put to a room full of professional economists at their annual conference, only 21.6% — about one in five — chose correctly, worse than the one in four you would expect from guessing among four options. Ferraro and Taylor published the result in 2005.

The answer that fails every time is the first one that comes to mind: “the ticket was free, so it cost nothing.”

⚠️ The two traps in one question: forgetting that the benefit of the alternative counts, and forgetting that its cost has to be netted out. Both show up on the quiz.

Instructor notes

Taken live, this works best when students commit to an answer before any discussion; the spread between $0 and $40 is the teaching material. Expect both to be popular. The 21.6% figure lands hard at the reveal, and it makes a room forgiving of itself before the ticket problem.

[Source note] Ferraro & Taylor (2005), “Do Economists Recognize an Opportunity Cost When They See One?” Cite it if asked; do not spend time on the study design.

§2.1Day 1

The $60 concert ticket

Work this one on paper before you click: write out what going is worth and what it costs tonight, then the same for staying home.

Predict first

You feel fine, you are just tired. Go, or stay home? Commit before you click.

Stay home — and the $60 has nothing to do with it.

Going is worth $45 and costs $28 tonight, for a net of $45 − $28 = +$17. Staying home is worth $40 and costs nothing tonight. $40 beats $17, so stay home.

The $60 ticket is a sunk cost: already spent, unrecoverable, and identical whichever you choose. Including it changes nothing except your ability to think clearly.

The pull you feel to go anyway — “but I paid for it” — has a name: the sunk-cost fallacy. Acting on it is expensive.

§2.1Day 1

Thinking at the margin: from here, what next?

Both problems in this part have the same shape. You are not asked should I have bought the ticket or was college a good idea. You are asked: given where I am right now, what should I do next? That question only ever looks forward.

✅ Counts in a marginal decision

  • Costs you will incur from here on
  • Benefits you will receive from here on
  • The value of the next best alternative, net of its own cost

✗ Does not count

  • Money already spent and unrecoverable
  • Time already invested
  • How you feel about having spent it

Where this is going: Part 2's supply curve is built out of exactly this logic. A seller deciding whether to produce one more unit compares what that one more unit will earn against what it will cost — not what the factory cost to build.

Marginal thinking asks one question: from here, what next? Everything behind you is scenery.
Instructor notes

This slide is the bridge from the ticket problem to the frontier and, in a bigger way, to Part 2. Say the forward link out loud: students who hear “this is how a supply curve gets built” arrive at supply expecting it.

§2.2Day 1

Build #1: why the frontier bows outward

A food truck can make tacos or burritos with the same kitchen and the same crew. Sketch what you think the frontier looks like before you click. (Week 1 asked you to guess straight or bowed. This build shows why.)

  1. Axes and units first. Tacos per hour on the vertical axis, 0 to 40. Burritos per hour on the horizontal, 0 to 10. Every point on the curve uses the whole kitchen — nobody is standing idle.
  2. The first trade is cheap. Going from 0 to 2 burritos per hour costs 2 tacos per hour (40 → 38). The crew shifts the people who were already best at rolling burritos.
  3. The middle trade costs more. Going from 4 to 6 burritos costs 6 tacos (34 → 28). The obvious burrito people are used up; now you are moving people who were pretty good at tacos.
  4. The last trade is expensive. Going from 8 to 10 burritos costs 10 tacos (20 → 10). You are now pulling your best taco cook off the taco line. Same +2 burritos; five times the price.
  5. That is the bow. Rising cost per unit is exactly what makes the line curve instead of running straight. Economists call it the law of increasing opportunity cost, and the reason behind it is simple: resources are not equally good at everything.
Step 1 of 5
The frontier bows because you use your best-suited resources first — and then run out of them.
Instructor notes

Sketch before build: expect straight lines — the useful wrong answer, and what Week 1 left them with.

The crew story is what makes it stick, so tell it in people terms, not resource terms: “who do you move first? Who do you move last?” Then name it.

§2.2Day 1

Reading increasing cost straight off the picture

How to read any bowed frontier

  • Take an equal step along the horizontal axis
  • Read the drop on the vertical axis
  • Do it again somewhere steeper — the drop is bigger
  • That growing drop is increasing opportunity cost

What a straight frontier would mean

Constant opportunity cost — every resource equally good at both jobs, so it never matters who you move. That takes a crew in which every worker is interchangeable between the two jobs; the moment some suit one job better than the other, the frontier bows.

Week 5's comparative-advantage frontiers are drawn straight on purpose, to keep the arithmetic clean; that module says so when it gets there.

Equal steps sideways, bigger and bigger drops down. That is the whole content of the bow.
Instructor notes

The same content as the build, as a static picture for the reading. Live, its value is the straight-line contrast and the Week 5 forward link, so nobody is blindsided when the frontiers go straight.

WARM-UPDay 2

The mowing question, answered

First, ninety seconds, no notes: write down everything you remember about the difference between a shift of a demand curve and a movement along one. Nothing is collected. The rule comes back, unchanged, later in this part.

At $10 the answer was no. At $60 it was yes. Nothing about the lawn changed — so what did?

Your opportunity cost of the afternoon did not change either. What changed is whether the payment beats it. Somewhere between $10 and $60 there is a number where you flip from no to yes. That number is your willingness to accept — the mirror image of the willingness to pay we used to build demand.

Buyers (Week 2)

Willingness to pay: the most you would hand over before you walk away.

Stack them from high to low → a demand curve, sloping down.

Sellers (this module)

Willingness to accept: the least you would take before you say no.

Stack them from low to high → a supply curve, sloping up.

Same idea, opposite direction. Buyers name a ceiling; sellers name a floor.
Instructor notes

Re-ask the mowing question cold before landing the willingness-to-accept framing. The symmetry with Week 2 is the thing to make explicit — students who see supply as “demand's mirror” have far less new material to learn.

§3.1Day 2

Supply is what sellers would offer at each price

Supply is the amount of a good or service producers are willing to produce and bring to market at each price. Same conditional grammar as demand: if the price were this, sellers would offer that much.

What a seller is really comparing. Producing one more unit uses resources — an hour, some materials, a machine's time — that could have gone somewhere else. A seller offers the unit when the price at least covers that: the cost of producing it at the margin. Which is Part 1's arithmetic, run by a firm instead of a person.

💰 Why higher prices bring more out

At $10/lawn only the people with nothing better to do say yes. At $60/lawn the payment starts beating the shift at work, the nap, the afternoon out. More people cross their own line.

🏭 The same story for a firm

A higher price makes it worth running the overtime shift, opening the second location, or drilling the well that was too expensive to bother with last year. Costlier units become worth producing.

The objection worth raising: sellers do not charge what a unit costs them; they charge what they can get. True — and the supply curve never claimed otherwise. It records the least each seller would take for each unit, the floor under the deal, not the price the deal settles at. What sellers actually get depends on the buyers too, and that is next week's question. This week you are drawing the floor.

⚠️ Watch out for: “sellers supply more at higher prices because they are greedy.” Greed is a constant — it does not explain a change. What changes is which units are worth producing once the price covers their cost.

Supply is what sellers would offer at each price — and the answer turns on cost, not character.
Instructor notes

The one line to land is the last watch-for: greed cannot explain a change because it does not vary. That distinction — constants cannot explain changes — is worth naming explicitly; it comes back at externalities and again at inflation.

§3.1Day 2

Build #2: turning the asks into a curve

Sketch the axes on paper first. Then walk the build. The numbers are illustrative: about thirty people asked the mowing question, and the count of those who would say yes at each price.

  1. Axes and units first — same convention as demand. Price in dollars per lawn on the VERTICAL axis, 0 to $60. Quantity in lawns per week on the HORIZONTAL axis, 0 to 28. Price stays on the vertical axis for supply too, because next week both curves share one picture.
  2. Plot the cheap end. At $10/lawn only 2 lawns get mowed — almost everyone's Saturday is worth more than that. At $20/lawn, 6 lawns.
  3. Plot the middle. $30/lawn → 11 lawns. $40/lawn → 17 lawns. Each price rise pulls in the people whose next-best afternoon was worth a little more.
  4. Plot the expensive end. $50/lawn → 22 lawns; $60/lawn → 26 lawns.
  5. Connect and name it S. The curve slopes UP — the mirror of last week. Read a trade off it: going from $20/lawn to $40/lawn brings out 17 lawns − 6 lawns = 11 more lawns. Nobody's preferences changed; the payment just cleared more people's opportunity cost.
Step 1 of 5

⚠️ The rule that does not bend: label your axes, with units. Price on the vertical, quantity on the horizontal — the same convention as demand, because next week these two curves go on the same picture.

§3.1Day 2

The law of supply — and the two reasons behind it

The law of supply: other things held constant, when the price of a good rises, the quantity supplied rises; when the price falls, the quantity supplied falls. Price and quantity supplied move in the same direction.

1 · More sellers clear their own bar

Every potential seller has a number — their opportunity cost. Raising the price does not change anyone's number; it just means more of those numbers are now beaten.

2 · Costlier units become worth making

For a firm, the cheap units get produced first. Producing more means overtime, older equipment, a less convenient site. A higher price makes those more expensive units worth making.

Checkpoint

The price of lumber doubles. According to the law of supply, what happens to the quantity of lumber supplied — and what happens to supply? Quantity supplied rises, supply rises, or both? Commit before you click.

Quantity supplied rises. Supply does not change at all.

At twice the price, mills that were not worth running an extra shift now are, so more lumber is offered — and you read that off the same curve, at a higher point. A change in the good's own price moves you along the supply curve. Supply — the whole curve — is untouched. It is the same trap as last week, on the other curve.

Price up, quantity supplied up. Same direction — and that is the only difference from demand.
Instructor notes

Harvest the two reasons from students before showing the cards, as with the two effects in Week 2.

On the checkpoint, “both” will get takers — that is the point. Resolve by pointing at the Week 2 rule: it did not change; only the curve did.

§3.1Day 2

Supply is the curve; quantity supplied is one point on it

📈 SUPPLY

The whole curve — every price paired with the quantity sellers would offer at it.

“Supply of lumber” = the whole line.

📍 QUANTITY SUPPLIED

One point on that curve. Meaningless unless you name the price.

“Quantity of lumber supplied at $4/board-foot” = one number.

Predict first · commit before you click

“A drought hit the wheat harvest, so the quantity of wheat supplied fell.” Right or wrong — and how would you fix it?

Wrong — and it is the mirror image of last week's error. A drought is not a change in the price of wheat. It is a natural condition: less wheat comes off the same fields whatever the price is, so it shifts the whole curve.

The fix: “A drought hit the wheat harvest, so the supply of wheat decreased — less wheat offered at every price.”

Notice the direction of the error is opposite to Week 2's. There, people said “demand” when they meant “quantity demanded.” Here they say “quantity supplied” when they mean “supply.” Same cure: name the variable that changed.

Supply is the curve. Quantity supplied is a point. Only the good's own price moves the point.
Instructor notes

Point out the symmetry explicitly — one rule rather than two, and it is true.

[Board] Write the wrong sentence and correct it in a second color, beside the Week 2 demand sentence if it is still up. Two corrections, one rule.

§3.1Day 2

From one seller to a whole market

Market supply is built exactly the way market demand was: add across at each price. Never add prices; every seller faces the same one.

Checkpoint

Kai would mow 3 lawns/week at $40/lawn and Lena would mow 5 lawns/week at $40/lawn. If they are the only two sellers, what is market quantity supplied at $40/lawn?

8 lawns/week. 3 lawns/week + 5 lawns/week = 8 lawns/week. Add the quantities at that price; the price itself is never added.

Market supply is every seller's supply, added across at each price.
Instructor notes

A straight transfer from Week 2; students get it fast. Point back at the mowing curve: adding across at each price is what built it.

§3.1Day 2 · reading

Gasoline — reading quantity supplied off the curve

One of the two official learning outcomes for ECON 100 names gasoline. Last week you read a gasoline demand curve; this is the supply side, drawn from a full supply schedule. Read it the same way: price first, then the quantity, then say both with their units.

Checkpoint

At $2.00 per gallon, how many gallons do sellers offer? Now the price falls to $1.40 per gallon. What happens to quantity supplied — and what happens to supply?

700 million gallons at $2.00 per gallon. When the price falls to $1.40, you slide down the same curve to 600 million gallons: quantity supplied falls by 100 million gallons. At the lower price, the wells and refineries that were only worth running at $2.00 stop, and the gallons they were producing come off the market.

Supply — the curve — has not moved. The price of gasoline itself changed, so this is a movement along the curve, and you read the new quantity off the same line. Said with the units: at $1.40 per gallon, sellers offer 600 million gallons.

Read the price, read the quantity, carry the units: 600 million gallons at $1.40 a gallon.
Instructor notes (reading-side slide)

This is the supply half of the SLO 1 gasoline example; Week 4 puts it on one graph with last week's gasoline demand curve. If a student raises it, one sentence: price first, quantity second, units on both.

§3.2Day 2

The Week 2 rule, transferred — word for word

Own price changed → MOVE along the curve.
Anything else changed → SHIFT the whole curve.

One thing to watch on the picture. A supply curve shifting right means more at every price, exactly as with demand. But because supply slopes up, a rightward shift also looks like the curve moving down. Do not let the visual talk you out of the words: right = more at every price, always.

Instructor notes

The down-and-right point is the one new visual idea in Part 2. It works drawn live: shift a supply curve right on the board and ask “did that go up or down?” Students will say down. Then say: “and what does it mean?” — more at every price. Insist on the words over the picture.

§3.2Day 2

The six things that shift a supply curve

The three that are really one idea

Input prices, technology and government policy all work through the same channel: they change what it costs to produce. Cheaper to make → more offered at every price → shift right.

The one that runs backwards

Expectations. A seller who expects a higher price later holds product back today — so supply today shifts left. That is the opposite of the buyer version, and it is the one to slow down on.

Six shifters. Most change what a unit costs to make; expectations is the one that runs backwards.
Instructor notes

Do not read the six aloud. Name each and take a lawn-mowing example from the room: what would be an input price for mowing? (Gas, a mower.) Technology? (A wider deck, a faster machine.) Policy? (A permit fee.) Natural conditions? (Rain all Saturday.) Six shifters, six student examples, boarded next to the curve.

Spend the time on expectations — it inverts relative to demand and it is on the quiz. Use the oil example: a producer who thinks prices spike next month leaves oil in the ground today.

§3.2Day 2

Shift or movement? Three scenarios, supply edition

Same rule as last week. For each scenario: name the variable → shift or movement → if a shift, direction as “more/less at every price.” Cover the right-hand column, commit to an answer for all three, then check.

The three scenarios — all in the market for lumber
#ScenarioThe trap it sets
1A new sawmill technology cuts processing time by a third. Warm-up. Technology, shift right — more at every price.
2Demand for new houses surges and the price of lumber climbs 40%. A price changed, and this time it IS lumber's own price. Movement along — the one that looks like a shift because a big story caused it.
3Mills expect lumber prices to be much higher next spring. The hard one. Expectations run backwards for sellers: hold product back, so supply today shifts LEFT.

The one question to ask yourself on every scenario: did you name the variable before you picked a direction?

HISTORYDay 2

Where this came from: an argument that lasted a century

For most of the 1800s economists were split on a question that now sounds odd: what gives something its value?

“Cost decides”

One camp said value comes from what it took to make a thing — the labor and resources poured into it. On this view, a diamond is expensive because it is hard to find and dig out.

“Usefulness decides”

The other camp said value comes from what a thing is worth to whoever wants it. On this view, water should be priceless and diamonds should be trinkets — which is plainly not what markets do.

Both camps were half right, and the argument was settled by a change of question. Stop asking “what is water worth?” and ask “what is one more glass of water worth, to someone who already has plenty?” At the margin, the puzzle dissolves. Cost sets the supply side; usefulness sets the demand side; a price comes out of the two together.

Which is exactly what next week does. The economist who put both curves on one diagram and made them settle a price — Alfred Marshall, 1890 — wrote the line the whole argument had been missing: asking whether cost or usefulness sets price is like asking which blade of the scissors does the cutting.

A century-long fight ended when someone asked about one more unit instead of the whole thing.
