MATH 1Day 1 · Tuesday

Percentage change

percentage change = (new − old) / old × 100

Gas rises from $4.00/gal to $4.50/gal:  ($4.50/gal − $4.00/gal) / $4.00/gal = 0.125 → +12.5%
Your hours drop from 20 hours/week to 16 hours/week:  (16 hours/week − 20 hours/week) / 20 hours/week = −0.20 → −20%

Units check: the top and bottom carry the same unit ($/gal over $/gal), so it cancels — a percentage change is a pure number, which is why we multiply by 100 to make it a percent.

Predict first

Gas goes from $4.00/gal to $4.50/gal. Is that a 12.5% rise or an 11.1% rise? Commit before revealing. (In class: fingers — 1 = 12.5%, 2 = 11.1%.)

+12.5%. Divide by the OLD price: ($4.50/gal − $4.00/gal) / $4.00/gal = 0.125. Dividing by the new $4.50/gal gives 11.1% — which is exactly how every price increase gets flattered. That's the trap, caught live.

Two traps: (1) Always divide by the OLD number — never the new one. (2) "20% OF $60" is $12. "A 20% increase FROM $60" is $72. Different questions.

Instructor notes (9:58 · 5 min)

Work the gas example on the board even though it's on the slide — the act of dividing by 4.00 (not 4.50) is the lesson. Ask: "Why does dividing by the new number flatter every price increase?" MATH 3–4Day 1 · Tuesday

Reciprocals, and units

Flip the fraction, flip the viewpoint

Predict first

In a small factory, making 1 more TV means giving up 2 bicycles. So 1 more bicycle costs… how many TVs? (In class: left hand = 2 TVs, right hand = half a TV.)

Half a TV. 1 TV costs 2 bicycles (2 bicycles/TV), so 1 bicycle costs 1/2 TV (0.5 TVs/bicycle). Flip the fraction, flip the point of view — same tradeoff, two directions. This exact move returns with opportunity cost (Week 3) and comparative advantage (Week 5).

Almost every number has units

"$15" means nothing yet. $15/hour? $15/day? $15 million? Half of all "math errors" in this course are unit errors. Almost every number you meet here carries a unit — write it next to the number and carry it through the math. The few that don't (a percentage, and later elasticity) are pure numbers, and knowing which kind you are holding is half the skill:

Add & subtract: one unit only.
3 bikes + 2 bikes = 5 bikes ✓ · 3 bikes + 2 TVs → can't combine ✗

Multiply & divide: units cancel or combine.
10 bikes × $15/bike = $150 — cross-cancel the "bikes."
In 240 miles / 8 gallons the units combine into 30 miles/gallon. If the leftover units aren't what you expected, the setup is wrong.

MATH 5Day 1 · Tuesday

Making & reading a chart — built one step at a time

Meet the campus coffee cart. We'll build its sales chart the way you should build (and read) every chart in this course — never all at once.

  1. Axes first. X = what we vary (the day of the week); Y = what we measure (cups sold). Label both — a chart with unlabeled axes is a picture, not a chart.
  2. Scale the Y axis, starting at zero. 0 to 240 cups. An axis that starts higher can make small changes look dramatic — check this on every chart you ever read.
  3. Plot one point and read it aloud. Monday: find Mon, go up to 120, across to the axis — "Monday, 120 cups." Reading a single point correctly is the core chart skill.
  4. Add the remaining days, one point at a time. Tue 150 · Wed 90 · Thu 150 · Fri 210 cups.
  5. Connect and title it. Now — and only now — describe the pattern: rise to Tuesday, sharp drop Wednesday, recovery Thursday, peak Friday.
Step 1 of 5

The four-question habit for READING any chart (same steps, in reverse): ① What's on each axis? ② What are the units and scale — does the Y axis start at zero? ③ Read ONE point. ④ Only then describe the pattern. Both chart questions on this week's quiz reward exactly this procedure.

Read every chart axes-first: what is measured, in what units, over what range.
Instructor notes (10:10 · 4 min)

WEEK 1Day 1 · Tuesday

What economics is (and isn't)

Predict first

"Economics is about money." Agree or disagree? Commit before revealing. (In class: hands up = agree — then convince your neighbor, and we vote again.)

Most hands go up — and that's the misconception this whole page dismantles. Money is one thing people economize; the subject is choice under scarcity. A family deciding how to spend a free Saturday afternoon is doing economics, and not a single dollar has to change hands.

Economics = the study of how people make choices under scarcity. Scarcity: wants exceed what resources — labor, tools, land, raw materials, and time — can deliver. For everyone, always. Even billionaires can't buy a 25th hour.

Money is the easy example, so here is the harder one. On August 22, 2026, regular gas averaged $5.60/gal in California against $4.10/gal nationwide — a $1.50/gal gap. That is a money fact. The economics is what Californians give up to close it: shorter trips, older cars kept longer, a job turned down because the commute stopped penciling out. Source: AAA Fuel Prices, August 22, 2026.

The sneakiest scarce resource is TIME — you are spending 85 scarce minutes on this class meeting, and Tuesday's workshop priced them at about $24 a seat. Families choose between the new car and the vacation; cities between police budgets and school budgets; nations between defense and the environment. Every one of those choices is economics in action.

Watch out for: "Economics = money, banking, and the stock market." That's the most common first-day mix-up (and a favorite quiz distractor). Money is just one tool a society uses to make tradeoffs easier to compare; it is not the subject.

Economics is the study of choice under scarcity. Money is one of the things being chosen about.

Why study economics?

Three reasons, and they'll come up all semester:

🌎 The world runs on it

Virtually every major problem — climate, poverty, conflict — has an economic dimension you can't see around. In July 2026, U.S. employers cut 23,000 jobs while unemployment held at 4.1 percent. Somebody has to explain how both are true at once.

🗳️ Citizenship requires it

When you vote on budgets, taxes, and regulations, you're voting on economics — as an income earner, a taxpayer, and a voter. Prices rose 3.4 percent over the year ending July 2026. Whether that is a crisis or a nuisance is a question you will be asked to vote on.

🧠 It sharpens your thinking

You'll be able to tell common sense from nonsense when politicians and pundits talk about the economy. On August 22, 2026, regular gas averaged $5.60/gal in California and $4.10/gal nationwide. Ask why, and you are already doing this.

Economics won't tell you what to think. It gives you the tools to see the choices — and their costs — for yourself.

WEEK 1Day 1 · Tuesday

The pin factory — Adam Smith, 1776

Pioneers built their own homes, grew their own food, repaired their own equipment. Almost nobody does that today — and we're richer for it. Smith's famous example says why.

Predict first

Ten workers split pin-making into 18 specialized operations instead of each making whole pins alone. How much more do they produce — about double, about 10×, or 100× or more? (In class: fingers — 1, 2, or 3.)

Hundreds-fold. One untrained worker alone: a handful of pins a day — Smith guessed twenty at the very most. Ten specialized workers: about 48,000 pins a day, or 4,800 per worker. The contrast, not the list of reasons, is the punchline.

Why specialization wins

  • Tasks match skills — it beats growing wheat at the beach and running hotels in North Dakota.
  • Repetition builds skill — specialists get quicker, better, and often invent improvements.
  • No switching costs, and scale enables machines — a factory making 50,000 cars a year can use assembly lines a 100-car shop never could. (Economies of scale: one sentence today, a full week in Week 6.)

The kicker: once you specialize, you MUST trade — the pin-maker eats no pins. Specialization and markets always come as a package deal. (That thread returns in Week 5 as comparative advantage.)

Thursday, we test Smith live. Four volunteers build "course cards" — a four-step product (fold, fold again, write ECON 100, star & ship). Today you vote on his claim; Thursday opens with the test. Round 1: each volunteer builds complete cards alone for 2 minutes. Round 2: same four, assembly-line style — one step each, pass it down — for 2 minutes. The class predicts the winner before Round 2, serves as quality control, and we compute both rounds' productivity on the board: cards / (4 workers × 2 minutes) = cards/worker-minute. Smith's reasons above stop being a list and become an explanation of your own data — including what a bottleneck does, which Smith knew too: the slowest station sets the line's pace.

Specializing raises output — and forces everyone who specializes to trade.

WEEK 1Day 1 · Tuesday

Where this idea came from

Adam Smith (1723–1790) was a Scottish moral philosopher — not an economist, because the job did not exist yet. He held the chair of moral philosophy at the University of Glasgow, and he was by all accounts an odd and likeable man: famously absent-minded, devoted to his mother, who he lived with for most of his life. He published The Wealth of Nations in 1776 — the same year as the Declaration of Independence, and the book usually counted as the beginning of economics as a field. It took him twelve years to write. His two biggest ideas are both on these slides: specialization makes nations rich, and — his famous "invisible hand" — millions of people pursuing their own interests through markets can end up coordinating with each other better than any planner could arrange on purpose.

Whether and when that's true is a question this course keeps testing all semester. Smith is the first stop in a running story: over the next 16 weeks you'll meet the economists who built, challenged, and rebuilt these ideas — usually by arguing with each other. (Thursday brings the first surprise: Smith's other book opens by insisting people care about each other's happiness.)

Portrait: etching after the James Tassie medallion, Wikimedia Commons, public domain.

Smith's claim was not that people are selfish. It was that specializing makes a nation richer.

WEEK 1Day 1 · Tuesday

Micro vs. macro: two zoom levels

Same economy, two lenses — like studying a lake fish-by-fish vs. as one ecosystem. The questions differ, not the subject. And both directions matter: a healthy overall economy changes who hires (macro → micro), and the whole economy is just millions of individual decisions added up (micro → macro).

Which lens you use depends on the question you're asking.
 Microeconomics · Weeks 1–9Macroeconomics · Weeks 10–16
What it studies Individual decision-makers and single markets: one household's budget, one firm's hiring, one industry's prices The economy's totals: growth (GDP), unemployment, inflation, interest rates — why whole economies boom, stall, recover
Example questions Why did rent in San Marcos rise? Should this shop hire a fourth barista? Why is California gas $5.60/gal when the national average is $4.10/gal (AAA, August 22, 2026)? Is the economy growing? Why did U.S. employers cut 23,000 jobs in July 2026 while unemployment stayed at 4.1 percent? Why did prices rise 3.4 percent over the year ending that month?
Policy levers — (studies individual markets and choices) Monetary policy (interest rates & lending — the Federal Reserve) · Fiscal policy (spending & taxes — Congress and the President)
Checkpoint

"Congress is debating a tax cut to boost overall employment." Micro or macro? Commit, then check.

Macro — it's about economy-wide employment and uses fiscal policy. "Why did my barista's wage rise?" would be micro.

WEEK 1Day 1 · Tuesday

Models: deliberately simple, on purpose

A subway map is "wrong" — no distances, no streets, no terrain. That's exactly why it works.

Economists analyze problems with theories — simplified representations of how a few variables relate. Applied to a specific situation, a theory is usually called a model (this course uses the words interchangeably). Keynes called economics "a method rather than a doctrine… a technique of thinking."

Models earn their keep by what they LEAVE OUT. An architect's tabletop model omits the plumbing — and shows how the building fits the block. Every model this semester is deliberately simplified. Our job is knowing what each one is FOR.

This is the standing answer to "but the real world is more complicated!" — which someone will (rightly) say by Week 4. Simplified models still answer questions and still get tested against evidence.

Models in this course are mostly graphs

Learning to read them is the core skill of the course — which is why the math primer ended with chart reading, and why Thursday's two big pictures (the budget constraint and the PPF) arrive finished, for you to read rather than build. Reading a model well is a skill in its own right, and it is this week's job.

First model, next slide: the circular flow diagram — the one picture that frames the whole course.

A model is judged by what it is for, not by what it leaves out.

WEEK 1Day 1 · Tuesday

Your first model: the circular flow — built loop by loop

  1. Two actors. Households (people) on the left; firms (businesses) on the right. Together, that's the whole society of this model — deliberately simple.
  2. The goods & services market (top). Firms deliver goods and services to households (A, solid); households send payment back (B, dashed, $). Who pays whom? Your money becomes their revenue.
  3. The labor market (bottom). Households provide labor to firms (C, solid); firms pay wages, salaries, and benefits back (D, dashed, $). In this market, the HOUSEHOLD is the seller.
  4. Follow the loop. Money (dashed flows B and D) circles one way; goods and labor (solid flows A and C) circle the other. One side's spending is always the other side's income — round and round. Later we can add government, banks, and trade to this picture.
Step 1 of 4
Predict first

In the labor market, who is the seller — firms or households? (In class: left hand = firms, right hand = households.)

Households. Households sell labor and buy goods; firms do the reverse — the two loops run in opposite directions. If that feels backwards, that's the point: "seller" depends on the market, not on who's bigger.

Who decides? How societies organize an economy

A modern economy is staggeringly complex. Nobody plans it, and yet in July 2026 roughly 164 million Americans held a job and the shelves stayed full. Who makes sure the number of TVs produced roughly matches the number people want? Societies have answered in a range of ways. Checkpoint

The U.S. has private firms — but also Social Security, public roads, and regulation. Which system is it?

A mixed economy. Nearly every real economy is. Every real economy is a mix. The question is where on the spectrum it sits.

WEEK 1Day 2 · Thursday

Opportunity cost: the real price of anything

The opportunity cost of any choice = the value of the next best alternative you gave up. Your warm-up answers just now WERE opportunity costs — nobody needed a definition first.

Sometimes the opportunity cost dwarfs the price tag. The out-of-pocket cost of college is tuition and books — but the full cost includes the wages you could be earning instead. That's why economists say college is a much bigger investment than the bill suggests — and why finishing matters.

Economists measure cost differently than accountants: not "what did you pay?" but "what did you give up?" — and "nothing" is never the alternative.

Try it: You spend $8 every workday buying lunch out; packing lunch costs $3. The opportunity cost of the restaurant habit is $5/day — about $1,250/year. Described as "$5 a day" it sounds cheap. Described as "a decent vacation, every year," it might change your behavior. Same cost, different frame.

Opportunity cost is the value of the next best thing you gave up — never zero, and rarely the price tag.
Instructor notes (9:48 · 2 min)

WEEK 1Day 2 · Thursday

“Rational” — the first working assumption

This is one of the four ideas this module is built on. It is also the single most misheard word in the subject.

What it means

People weigh costs and benefits as they see them, with the information they actually have, and choose what they believe makes them better off.

Note every hedge in that sentence. As they see them. Information they have. Believe. None of it claims anyone is right.

What it does NOT mean

Not a compliment. Not “always correct.” Not “cold” or “calculating.” Not a claim that people never err.

It is a modeling assumption — the price of getting predictions out of a model at all.

Watch out for: hearing “rational” as a judgment about people — the biggest landmine of Week 1 and a favourite quiz distractor. And when real people bend the rule in systematic ways — sunk-cost behaviour, two slides ahead — that becomes the next thing economists study, not proof the method is broken.

Predict first

Someone buys a lottery ticket. Is that irrational? Commit before revealing.

Not necessarily. If they are buying two minutes of daydreaming for a dollar and know the odds, the assumption holds fine. It only breaks if they believe the odds are good — and that is a claim about information, not about their character.

Rational is an assumption about how people choose, not a compliment about how well they choose.
Instructor notes (9:50 · 5 min)

Slow down here — rationality carries its own slide because it is the idea students most often misread. Defuse the word first, then give the modeling defence: assumptions buy predictions. Run the lottery-ticket predict as a hands-up vote. Objections get their own slide at 10:49 — say “parked, on the agenda” out loud.

WEEK 1Day 2 · Thursday

“Self-interested” — the second working assumption

What it means

People pursue their own goals — and those goals routinely include family, friends, community, and causes they care about.

A parent working a double shift for their kids is acting in self-interest. So is someone donating a kidney. The word says whose goals, not which goals.

What it does NOT mean

Not selfishness. Not greed. Not that people only care about money — the assumption is silent on what anyone wants.

The founder said it first. Adam Smith — the economist the world remembers for “self-interest” — opened his other book, The Theory of Moral Sentiments (1759), with this sentence: “How selfish soever man may be supposed, there are evidently some principles in his nature, which interest him in the fortune of others, and render their happiness necessary to him, though he derives nothing from it except the pleasure of seeing it.” Self-interest was never an accusation of greed — the field's founder said so in his first sentence, seventeen years before The Wealth of Nations. (Quoted in OpenStax Principles of Economics 3e, §2.3; the book is public domain.)

Objections like “but people aren't rational!” are real and get their own slide near the end of today — parked, not dodged.

Self-interest means pursuing your own goals — and your goals include people you love.

The Smith quote is the whole slide — read it aloud, slowly. Tie it back to Tuesday's pin factory: same man, and he wrote the sympathy book first. Then the one-line bridge: two assumptions in hand, we can ask what every economy has to decide.

WEEK 1Day 2 · Thursday

The economic problem: three questions every society answers

The fourth of the four — and the question the rest of this course keeps answering in different ways.

WHAT gets produced? HOW is it produced? FOR WHOM?

Scarcity is why the questions exist. If resources were unlimited, every society would simply produce everything for everyone and there would be nothing to decide. The economic problem is scarcity turned into three decisions.

Checkpoint · out loud

Housing in San Diego: answer the three questions in your own words.

WHAT: apartments vs. single-family homes — and how many of each get built. HOW: density, labour, land, and building rules. FOR WHOM: who can afford what gets built — and who gets priced out to Temecula.

What, how, and for whom. Every economy in history has answered these three.
Instructor notes (10:00 · 4 min)

Fast and oral — no board work. Lead with the scarcity link: the three questions exist BECAUSE resources are limited. San Diego housing makes it concrete. No answers to defend.

WEEK 1Day 2 · Thursday

Same three questions — three different machineries

Tuesday's systems were not a separate topic. They are three answers to the questions on the last slide.

Traditional

WHAT: what was produced last year. HOW: the way it has always been done. FOR WHOM: by custom, kinship and role.

Stable and predictable; slow to change and slow to grow.

Command

WHAT: whatever the plan specifies. HOW: as the plan directs. FOR WHOM: as the planners allocate.

Can move enormous resources fast; struggles to know what people actually want.

Market

WHAT: whatever people will pay for. HOW: whatever is cheapest. FOR WHOM: whoever can pay.

Responsive and inventive; indifferent to whether the answer is fair.

Every real economy is mixed. The United States answers mostly through markets, with command elements (public schools, the military, Medicare) and traditional ones (unpaid family care) doing real work alongside. The interesting question is never which system — it is which mix, for which question.

Systems are not ideologies to pick between. They are machineries for answering what, how, and for whom.

WEEK 1Day 2 · Thursday

Thinking at the margin

Most choices aren't all-or-nothing. They're one-more-or-one-less.

Predict first

Third slice of pizza: worth more, the same, or less to you than the first? (In class: fingers — 1 more, 2 same, 3 less.)

Less — every time. The price of each slice is constant, but each slice is worth less to you than the last. Falling marginal benefit against a constant marginal cost IS the stopping rule: when the extra benefit drops below the extra cost — stop.

Study hours

The 4th hour on econ vs. the 1st hour on history: allocate where the MARGINAL hour pays most. And an hour has a market price — California's minimum wage is $16.90/hour as of January 1, 2026 — so every study hour is being weighed against something real. That's why spreading hours across courses beats perfecting one — and it quietly previews diminishing returns (Week 3's lumberjacks, Week 6's cost curves).

The overtime shift

The wage is the same every hour — but each successive hour costs more (fatigue, the evening you're missing). There's a rational stopping point even at a good wage. (HW1 Part A's example scenario echoes this.)

Vocabulary: marginal benefit · marginal cost · thinking at the margin — compare the NEXT unit's benefit to the NEXT unit's cost.

Decide one-more-or-one-less. Totals are the wrong unit for a decision.
Instructor notes (10:05 · 11 min for this slide + next)

Pizza first because everyone has lived it (PREDICT-VOTE-RESOLVE #2 — run the finger vote). Board the three vocabulary terms as you say them. Then the two applications students actually face.

WEEK 1Day 2 · Thursday

Sunk costs: the money that's already gone

Sunk costs are money and time already spent that you can't get back. Paid $12 for a movie that's awful 30 minutes in? The $12 is gone whether you stay or leave. The only real question is: what's the best use of the next 90 minutes? At California's $16.90/hour minimum wage as of January 1, 2026, those 90 minutes are worth about $25 — twice what the ticket cost.

Rational decisions look forward, not back. The lesson of sunk costs is to ignore them — compare only what changes from here on.

This is also your first example of a systematic deviation: real people honor sunk costs constantly ("I already paid for it!"). That predictable bending of the rationality assumption is exactly the kind of thing economists study — remember the parked objection. The field that studies these predictable deviations has a name — behavioral economics — and it grew out of exactly the observation you just made.

Money already spent should not appear anywhere in a decision about what to do next.
Instructor notes (~2 min, inside the margin block)

Sunk cost got its definition in the reading; here it needs the movie example + the flowchart + the forward-looking rule. HW1 Part A asks them to find a sunk cost in their own decision. The "systematic deviation" line plants the 10:32 objections segment.

WEEK 1Day 2 · Thursday

Three models, one method

Tuesday we said models are simple on purpose. Here is the habit that makes a model useful instead of misleading — three questions you ask of any model, every time.

What does it show?

The one relationship the model is built to make visible. If you cannot say it in a sentence, you have not read the model yet.

What does it assume?

The things held still so that one relationship can be seen. Assumptions are not cheating — they are the price of seeing anything at all.

What does it leave out?

Everything else. A model is judged by whether it is useful for its question, never by whether it is complete.

Worked example — the circular flow (Tuesday, and now through the frame): shows that households and firms are each other's customers, so income and spending are the same flow seen twice. Assumes two sectors and no leaks — no government, no banks, no imports. Leaves out almost everything, including the entire financial system. It is still the picture that explains why one person's spending cut is another person's lost income.

Read a model the way you read a subway map: ask what it is for, not what it is missing.

Thursday, in pairs. You will be handed the next two pictures and asked exactly these three questions. Nothing to draw, nothing to solve.

WEEK 1Day 2 · Thursday

Picture #1: the budget constraint — one person against scarcity

What it shows

Every combination one person can afford on a fixed budget — and that choosing more of one thing means less of the other. The line's steepness is the trade: one burger costs four bus tickets.

What it assumes

The budget is fixed, both prices are fixed, and there are only two things to buy. Change the budget and the line moves outward without tilting — more money, same trade. Change a price and the line tilts, because the trade itself has changed.

What it leaves out

Saving, borrowing, everything else Alphonso might want, and any reason he prefers one point over another. It shows what is affordable, never what is best.

You will operate this model in Week 4. This week the job is to read it.

The budget line shows what you can just afford. Its steepness is the trade your prices force.
Instructor notes (10:10 · 6 min)

Do NOT build the line. It arrives finished. Walk the three cards, then ask the room for the two thought experiments orally — budget doubles (parallel shift, trade unchanged) versus burger price doubles (tilt, trade changed). Both answers land in one minute; resist drawing them.

WEEK 1Day 2 · Thursday

Picture #2: the PPF — a whole society against scarcity

What it shows

The combinations a society can produce at full stretch. On the curve is efficient; inside (point R) means resources are idle and more of both goods is available for free; outside (point N) is out of reach today.

What it assumes

Two goods, fixed resources, fixed technology, and everything usable actually being used. The curve bows because resources are not interchangeable — that is the law of increasing opportunity cost, and it gets its full treatment in Week 3.

What it leaves out

Which point society should pick. The PPF is a menu of what is possible; choosing among the options is a normative question, and no curve answers it.

You will operate this model in Week 11, where this course puts the production-possibilities curve to work. This week: read it.

Inside the frontier is waste. On it is efficient. Outside is out of reach, for now.
Instructor notes (10:16 · 6 min)

Point R is the whole slide — idle resources mean a free lunch is genuinely available, which surprises students who have been told there are none. Then the last card: the PPF says nothing about which point to choose. That hands straight to positive vs. normative next slide.

WEEK 1Day 2 · Thursday

Positive vs. normative: IS vs. OUGHT

POSITIVE — what IS

"The minimum wage reduced teen employment in city X."

Testable. Could be false. Evidence settles it.

NORMATIVE — what OUGHT to be

"The minimum wage should be $20."

A values claim. Economics clarifies the stakes; it can't settle OUGHT by itself.

Predict first

"The minimum wage should be $20" — IS claim or OUGHT claim? And the teen-employment sentence? (In class: left hand = IS, right hand = OUGHT.)

"Should" makes it an OUGHT (normative) claim — evidence alone cannot settle it. The teen-employment sentence is an IS (positive) claim: checkable, and it could turn out to be false. Same topic, two different kinds of claim — that's the cut.

The course habit: reading the news, sort the claims into IS and OUGHT first. That's exactly what HW1c (Economic Naturalist #1) asks — claim, tool, evidence, limits — and you'll use it every single week of this course.

A positive claim can be checked. A normative claim is a value judgment. Data settles only the first.
Instructor notes (10:43 · 6 min)

The pair of minimum-wage sentences makes the cut cleanly — same topic, different kind of claim. PREDICT-VOTE-RESOLVE #4: hands vote, rerun once with the teen-employment sentence so the pair is complete. The habit line is the bridge to HW1 Part B's four-part format.

WEEK 1Day 2 · Thursday

The objections, head-on — as promised

"People aren't rational."

Often true — in specific, systematic ways (you met one today: sunk-cost behavior), and economists study exactly those deviations. But picture a basketball player throwing a bounce pass: a physicist could work out the correct speed and trajectory — the player just throws, and if they're good, accurately. That a good player doesn't do the physics doesn't make the physics wrong — and that people don't consciously calculate doesn't make the model wrong. It predicts what skilled choosers actually do.

"Economics is heartless."

Making tradeoffs visible is not the same as ignoring values. Hiding tradeoffs helps no one — least of all the people the tradeoff lands on.

And self-interest includes the freedom to choose what to work at, buy, and give: people use that freedom to pick high-paying careers — or charity, teaching, social work, and time with family. In OpenStax's words, "people's freedom to make their own economic choices has a moral value worth respecting."

Skepticism is welcome here. Bring it all sixteen weeks — economics gets better when you argue with it.
