What this week covers

In Week 6, a competitive market produced the efficient quantity. In Week 7, a monopoly produced less than that quantity. This week, we look at the other ways a market can produce the wrong quantity, and at what the government does about each one. A market fails when, left alone, it produces a quantity other than the efficient quantity.

Day 1: when one market fails

We follow paper mills that pollute a river and a town downstream that pays for the harm. We measure how much paper the market makes and compare it with the efficient quantity. Then we compare three ways a government responds. We finish with goods that benefit people who do not pay for them, and with goods that a market does not provide at all.

Day 2: when the whole economy fails

We move from one market to the whole economy. We look at mass unemployment, unstable prices, and the business cycle. We use two real cases, the Great Depression and the recession of 2008–09. We end with the argument over whether an economy returns to full employment on its own.

RETRIEVALDay 1

90 seconds, no notes: when is a market efficient?

From memory, write what made the Week 6 sweetcorn market efficient in the long run. Then write what the Week 7 monopoly roaster did to the quantity of coffee, and what that cost the town.

Predict first

Paper mills on the Arden River release wastewater into the river. The town of Millbrook, downstream, spends $200 to clean the water for every ton of paper the mills make, and its fishing boats catch less. Does the price of a ton of paper include that $200? Write one sentence before you read on.

No. The mills pay for wood, labor, and energy, and those costs are in the price of paper. The mills do not pay Millbrook's water bill, so that cost is not in the price.

So the buyers of paper and the mills make their trade as if the $200 did not exist. Day 1 is about costs and benefits like that one, which land on people who are not part of the trade.

A price includes the costs the seller pays. It leaves out the costs that land on someone else.

MARKET FAILUREDay 1

Four ways a single market can fail

A market failure is a market that, left alone, produces a quantity other than the efficient quantity. The efficient quantity is the quantity at which the value of one more unit to everyone it affects equals its cost to everyone it affects. You met the first source in Week 7.

Four sources of market failure, what goes wrong, an example, and the usual government response
SourceWhat the market doesExampleUsual government response
Market power (Week 7)Produces too little, at too high a priceA town's only water utilityAntitrust law. For a natural monopoly, a public utilities commission sets the price.
Negative externalityProduces too muchPaper mills that pollute a riverA rule, a tax, or tradable permits
Positive externalityProduces too littleFlu shotsA subsidy
Public goodProduces too little, or none at allA tornado sirenThe government provides it and pays with taxes.

Watch for: treating a market failure as proof that a government program will do better. A market failure is a reason to consider a government response. Whether a particular response does better than the market is a separate question, and it has its own evidence.

Each source of market failure leads the market to the wrong quantity, and each one has a different usual response.

This table is the map for Day 1. The first row closes the Week 7 question about who decides what a water utility charges. In California, the answer is the California Public Utilities Commission for privately owned water and power companies, and a city council for a city-owned utility.

Say the watch-for. It keeps the positive question (does the market fail?) apart from the normative one (what should the government do?).

EXTERNALITYDay 1

Negative externality: the mills and the town downstream

Making a ton of paper costs the mills their own wood, labor, and energy. It also costs Millbrook $200 a ton in water treatment and lost fishing. Millbrook is not the buyer of the paper and not the seller. Millbrook is a third party to the trade.

Private cost

The private cost is the cost the producer pays. The mills' supply curve shows their private cost of each ton.

External cost

The external cost is the cost that falls on third parties. Here, it is $200 a ton, paid by Millbrook.

Social cost

The social cost is the full cost to everyone: private cost + external cost. Every ton costs society $200 more than it costs the mills.

An externality is a cost or a benefit of a market activity that falls on a third party, who is not paid for the harm or charged for the benefit. An external cost is a negative externality. An external benefit is a positive externality.

Checkpoint — the third-party test

The test has two questions. Does the activity affect someone who is not the buyer or the seller? Is that person paid for the harm, or charged for the benefit? Apply the test to the mills and Millbrook.

Yes, and no. The wastewater affects Millbrook, which is not part of the trade. Nobody pays Millbrook for the harm.

So the activity passes the test, and there is a negative externality of $200 a ton. If the mills paid Millbrook $200 a ton, the second answer would change to yes, and the cost would be part of the mills' private cost.

An externality is a cost or a benefit that lands on someone outside the trade and that nobody pays for.
Instructor notes

Run the two questions on two more cases from the class: a neighbor's loud party (yes, and no), and a restaurant that buys its fish from a fishing boat (no third party, so no externality).

The vocabulary order on this slide is the order the quiz uses: private cost, external cost, social cost.

EXTERNALITYDay 1

Build #1: how much paper the market makes, and the efficient quantity

  1. Draw the axes and label the units first. Paper, in thousand tons per week, goes on the horizontal axis. Dollars per ton go on the vertical axis.
  2. Demand. Buyers will pay $900 a ton for the first tons. Each additional thousand tons sells for $100 a ton less.
  3. Supply, and what the market does. The supply curve shows the mills' own cost of each ton. Demand meets supply at 4 thousand tons a week and $500 a ton. This is what the market produces when it is left alone.
  4. Social cost. Each ton also costs Millbrook $200. Add $200 to the mills' own cost at every quantity. The social cost curve lies $200 above the supply curve.
  5. The efficient quantity. Demand meets social cost at 3 thousand tons a week and $600 a ton. Up to 3 thousand tons, each ton is worth more to its buyer than it costs everyone. Beyond 3 thousand tons, each ton costs more than it is worth.
  6. The deadweight loss. The market makes 1 thousand tons too many. For those tons, the cost to society, $600 to $700 a ton, is more than their value to buyers, $500 to $600 a ton. The shaded triangle is the deadweight loss: ½ × 1 thousand tons × $200 a ton = $100 thousand a week.
Step 1 of 6

With a negative externality, the market quantity is where demand meets supply, and the efficient quantity is where demand meets social cost. The market produces too much, because the producers do not pay the external cost.

The market makes 4 thousand tons. The efficient quantity is 3 thousand tons. The difference costs $100 thousand a week of deadweight loss.
Instructor notes

Draw-it-yourself first: before step 4, ask pairs to sketch where the social cost line goes. The common error is drawing it below supply.

At step 6, compare the triangle with Week 7's. The monopoly's triangle came from too little output. This triangle comes from too much output. Both are value lost because the quantity is wrong.

EXTERNALITYDay 1

The efficient amount of pollution is not zero

At the market quantity of 4 thousand tons, Millbrook bears $200 × 4 thousand tons = $800 thousand a week of harm. At the efficient quantity of 3 thousand tons, Millbrook still bears $200 × 3 thousand tons = $600 thousand a week of harm.

Why the harm does not go to zero

The first 3 thousand tons of paper are worth more to buyers than they cost everyone, including Millbrook. Making no paper at all would remove the harm, but it would also remove all of that value. The efficient quantity keeps the tons that are worth their full cost and drops the tons that are not.

What the market gets wrong

The market's error is the 4th thousand tons. Those tons are worth $500 to $600 a ton to buyers, but they cost $600 to $700 a ton once the harm is counted. That error is $100 thousand a week, not the full $800 thousand.

Checkpoint — commit before you reveal

A city council member says: “The mills cause harm, so the efficient policy is to shut them down.” Does the graph support that claim?

No. On the graph, the first 3 thousand tons are worth more than their full cost. Shutting the mills down would lose that value, which is far more than the $100 thousand a week of deadweight loss.

The graph supports making less paper. It does not support making no paper. Whether a community should accept any pollution at all is a normative question, and the graph does not settle it.

The efficient quantity counts the harm. It does not remove all of the harm.
Instructor notes

This is the slide students push back on. Let them. Then ask what would have to be true about the harm for the efficient quantity to be zero: the external cost would have to be large enough that the social cost curve starts at $900 or more, where demand starts. Here, that takes an external cost of $800 a ton or more.

POLICYDay 1

Two responses: a rule, or a tax per ton

The first response is command-and-control regulation: a rule that limits each mill's pollution or requires a particular technology. The second response is a corrective tax, also called a Pigouvian tax: a tax on each unit equal to the external cost.

What the $200 tax does

The tax raises the mills' cost of each ton by $200, so the supply curve shifts up to the social cost curve. The quantity falls to 3 thousand tons, the efficient quantity. Buyers pay $600 a ton, and the mills keep $400 a ton. The government collects $600 thousand a week of tax revenue.

Why a tax and not only a rule

With the tax, each mill decides for itself how much to cut. A mill that can cut its pollution cheaply has a reason to cut more, to pay less tax. A rule gives every mill the same limit, whether its cuts are cheap or expensive.

A corrective tax makes the producers count the harm as part of their own cost.
Instructor notes

Point out that the tax does not need to know the efficient quantity in advance. It needs to know the external cost per ton, and the market finds the quantity.

A student will ask what happens to the $600 thousand. Say that the model does not decide that. The government could pay Millbrook, cut another tax, or spend it elsewhere, and that choice is a separate question.

POLICYDay 1

The third response: tradable permits, or cap and trade

With tradable permits, the government sets a cap on total pollution, issues that many permits, and lets firms buy and sell them. Two power plants each emit 10 tons of sulfur dioxide a day. The cap is 10 tons a day, so each plant receives 5 permits, and the two plants must cut 10 tons between them.

The cost of cutting 10 tons a day: the same cut for each plant, or a trade of permits at $400 each
PlantCost to cut one tonSame cut for each plantWith trading at $400 a permit
North$200Cuts 5 tons: $1,000 a dayCuts all 10 tons ($2,000) and sells 5 permits for $2,000: $0 a day
South$600Cuts 5 tons: $3,000 a dayCuts nothing and buys 5 permits: $2,000 a day
Both plantsCost of the cut: $4,000 a dayCost of the cut: $2,000 a day

What trading does

The same 10 tons are cut, and the air is the same. The cut is made by the plant that can make it for $200 a ton instead of $600 a ton. Each plant is $1,000 a day better off than under the same-cut rule. Any permit price between $200 and $600 gives both plants a reason to trade.

A real program

The Acid Rain Program, created by the Clean Air Act Amendments of 1990, used this design for sulfur dioxide from power plants. Trading began in 1995. Each permit, called an allowance, lets a plant emit one ton.

The cap sets the total cut. With trading, the plant with the lowest cost of cutting has a reason to make most of the cut.

Ask who gains from the trade. Both plants do, by $1,000 a day each, and the $2,000 a day saving is the sum.

EXTERNALITYDay 1

Positive externality: the market makes too little

A flu shot at a pharmacy costs $40. Maria values her own protection at $30, so she does not get the shot. But her shot would also protect her coworkers and her family. That protection is worth $25 to them, and they do not pay for it.

Predict first

Count the value to everyone the shot affects. Is Maria's flu shot worth its $40 cost?

Yes. The shot is worth $30 to Maria and $25 to the people around her, which is $55 in total. That is more than the $40 it costs. But Maria counts only her own $30, so she skips it.

In other words, the $25 of protection for other people is an external benefit. Maria does not receive it, so it does not enter her decision. With a positive externality, the market produces too little.

The usual response: a subsidy

A subsidy is a payment from the government that lowers the price a buyer pays or raises the price a seller receives. A $25 subsidy, equal to the external benefit, lowers Maria's price to $15. The shot is worth $30 to her, so she now has a reason to get it.

Other examples

Education benefits the student and also the employers, neighbors, and voters around the student. Basic research benefits the firms that can use what it discovers, not only the lab that paid for it. Both receive government support, partly for this reason.

A negative externality leads to too much. A positive externality leads to too little.
Instructor notes

Work the case in this order: Maria's own comparison ($30 against $40), then the total ($55 against $40), then the subsidy ($30 against $15).

The common error is to say that the subsidy is a gift to Maria. The point of the subsidy is the $25 of protection it buys for other people.

PUBLIC GOODSDay 1

Public goods: why a market provides too little

Two questions sort every good. Is it rival: does one person's use leave less for others? Is it excludable: can the seller keep someone who has not paid from using it?

Four kinds of goods, sorted by whether they are rival and whether they are excludable
RivalryExcludableNot excludable
RivalPrivate good: a pizzaCommon resource: fish in the ocean
Not rivalClub good: a streaming servicePublic good: a tornado siren

The levee

A river town has 100 households. Each household values a levee against floods at $50, so the levee is worth $5,000 to the town. It costs $3,000 to build. The levee is worth building. But every household is protected whether or not it pays, so each one has a reason to let the others pay. A household that benefits without paying is a free rider, and voluntary payments fall short of $3,000.

The usual response

The town pays for the levee with a tax of $30 on each household. Each household gets $50 of protection for $30, so each one is $20 better off. National defense, street lighting, and weather warnings are paid for the same way.

A public good is nonrival and nonexcludable. A common resource is rival but not excludable, so it tends to be overused: each boat has a reason to catch more fish before another boat does.

Instructor notes

Ask the class to place two more goods in the table: a public park that is often crowded (rival when crowded, not excludable) and a toll road (excludable, and rival when traffic is heavy). Both are arguable. Say so instead of forcing them into one cell.

HISTORYDay 1

Where this idea came from: Arthur Pigou, 1920

📖 The idea

Arthur Pigou was an economist at the University of Cambridge in England. In The Economics of Welfare, published in 1920, he argued that a producer's private cost can differ from the cost to society. One of his examples was the damage that sparks from railway engines did to the woods beside the tracks, which the railways did not pay for.

💰 The proposal

Pigou proposed a tax on the activity equal to the harm it does to others. That is the corrective tax from the earlier slide, and it carries his name: the Pigouvian tax. The idea came decades before modern environmental law in the United States.

Checkpoint — positive or normative?

Two statements: A. “A $200 tax per ton lowers the quantity of paper to 3 thousand tons a week.” B. “The government should tax the mills $200 a ton.” Which one can the graph settle?

Statement A is a positive claim. It follows from the demand curve and the supply curve, and it is either right or wrong.

Statement B is a normative claim. It depends on more than the graph: whether the $200 estimate of the harm is right, who should pay for the harm, and what the government does with the revenue.

Pigou's idea was to put a price on the harm, so that the producer counts it.
Instructor notes

One minute on Pigou. The locomotive example is his own, and it lets students see that the idea is older than any current political debate about it.

MACRO FAILURESDay 2

From one market to the whole economy

On Day 1, each failure was inside one market, and the other markets worked. Day 2 asks what happens when many markets fall short at the same time. The study of the whole economy is macroeconomics, and it is the subject of Weeks 10–16.

Mass unemployment

Many people who want to work at the going wage cannot find a job. The goods and services they would have made are never made. The economy is inside its production possibilities frontier, the Week 1 model.

Unstable prices

Inflation is a rise in the general level of prices. Deflation is a fall in the general level of prices. Either one changes what money is worth. That change can shift gains and losses between the two sides of a contract written in dollars.

Booms and busts

Total output rises and falls in a pattern called the business cycle. The pattern has four parts: an expansion, a peak, a recession, and a trough, where the decline stops. A recession is a significant decline in economic activity that spreads across the economy and lasts more than a few months.

The unemployment rate is the percentage of the labor force that is unemployed. The labor force is the people who have a job and the people who are looking for one.

A failure of the whole economy is output below what the economy can produce, or a general price level that rises or falls sharply.
Instructor notes

Name the hinge out loud: Day 2 is the course’s first look at macroeconomics. Week 10 measures unemployment and inflation properly. Today only needs the definitions.

MACRO FAILURESDay 2

Unemployment is a surplus of labor

In one region, 10 thousand people want work at $20 an hour, and firms hire all 10 thousand. Then people and businesses cut their spending, and firms need fewer workers at every wage. The demand for labor shifts left.

At the old wage of $20 an hour

Firms hire 8 thousand workers, and 10 thousand people want work. The surplus of 2 thousand workers is unemployment: 2 thousand of 10 thousand, or 20 percent of the people who want work. This is the surplus from Week 4, where a price above equilibrium left sellers with more than buyers wanted.

If the wage fell to $19 an hour

The surplus would be gone, and 9 thousand people would be working. The question for the rest of Day 2 is whether wages fall that way in a whole economy, and how long it takes.

Unemployment is a surplus of labor at the going wage.
Instructor notes

Draw the Week 4 price-floor picture beside this one. Students recognize the surplus at once, and the only new step is that the price is a wage.

Do not resolve the $19 question here. It is the subject of the Say and Keynes slide.

HISTORYDay 2

Does an economy recover on its own? Two answers

📖 Say's law, 1803

The French economist Jean-Baptiste Say argued that producing goods creates the income to buy them. His view is often summarized as “supply creates its own demand.” On this view, a surplus of labor leads wages to fall until the surplus is gone, and the economy returns to full employment on its own.

📖 Keynes, 1936

The British economist John Maynard Keynes published The General Theory of Employment, Interest and Money in 1936. He argued that wages fall slowly. He also argued that when wages do fall, workers spend less, and firms then need even fewer workers. On this view, total spending can stay too low for years, and government spending can make up the shortfall.

Checkpoint — what the evidence settled, and what it did not

Look at the 1929–1942 chart again. Which part of the argument can the data settle?

The data settle a positive question. Unemployment stayed above 14 percent from 1931 through 1940. So, in the 1930s, the U.S. economy did not return to full employment quickly on its own.

The data do not settle a normative question. How large a role the government should take in managing the economy depends on judgments about risk, cost, and freedom. Economists still disagree about it, and Week 14 compares the main schools of thought.

Say's law says the economy corrects itself. Keynes argued that it can stay below full employment for years.
Instructor notes

Present both views as evenly as you can. Say's law describes the long run well in many periods. Keynes was answering the 1930s, when the long run took a decade.

The predict question from the Day 1 wrap resolves here. Ask for the sentences students wrote before showing either card.

CASE 2Day 2

The recession of 2008–09

The recession began in December 2007 and ended in June 2009. It started in the housing market and spread through the banks to the rest of the economy.

What happened

The unemployment rate doubled, from 5.0 percent in December 2007 to 10.0 percent in October 2009. Real GDP fell by about 2.5 percent from 2007 to 2009. Consumer prices fell by 0.4 percent in 2009, the first fall in a year since 1955.

What the government did

The Federal Reserve cut its target interest rate to near zero in December 2008. In February 2009, Congress passed the American Recovery and Reinvestment Act, which increased federal spending and cut taxes. Deposit insurance meant that insured depositors had no reason to rush to withdraw their money. There was no wave of thousands of bank suspensions like the one in the 1930s.

Checkpoint — commit before you reveal

The 2008–09 recession was far smaller than the Depression. Does that prove the government's response made it smaller?

No, not by itself. The comparison the question needs is with what would have happened without the response, and nobody can observe that. The two slumps also started from different shocks.

Economists compare cases, regions, and timing to estimate the effect, and they still disagree about its size. That is a disagreement about evidence, not only about values.

Unemployment doubled in 2008–09. Whether it would have been worse without the response cannot be read off the chart.
Instructor notes

This checkpoint is the same reasoning as Week 7's Standard Oil question: a result compared with the past is different from a result compared with what would otherwise have happened.

The 1955 comparison uses annual average consumer prices; 2009 was the first calendar year with a lower average price level than the year before since 1955.

LIMITSDay 2

Three questions the models cannot answer

💧 How big is the harm?

The paper graph used $200 a ton for Millbrook's harm. In a real case, that number has to be estimated, and the estimate can be wrong. The right tax is only as good as that estimate.

⚖️ Who should pay?

A tax puts the cost on the mills and their buyers. A payment to the mills of $200 for each ton they do not make puts it on taxpayers. Both can bring the market to 3 thousand tons. Which one is fair is a normative question.

🏛️ How large a role?

A market failure shows that the market gets the quantity wrong. It does not show that a particular government program will get it right. Governments can also fail, and that is a question for evidence.

Checkpoint — the one to get right

Two statements: A. “From 1929 to 1933, the unemployment rate rose from 3.2 percent to 24.9 percent.” B. “The government should guarantee a job to everyone who wants one.” Which statement can the data settle?

Statement A is a positive claim. It can be checked against the Bureau of Labor Statistics data, and it is either right or wrong.

Statement B is a normative claim. It depends on judgments about cost, freedom, and fairness. The data can inform it, for example by measuring how much a program costs, but the data cannot settle it.

Economics measures the failure and compares the responses. Choosing a response also takes a judgment that the numbers do not make.
Instructor notes

Three cards, one minute each, with no editorializing. Present both halves of the checkpoint as evenly as you can.

WATCH FORWrap-up

Six common errors this week

Common Module 8 errors and the correction for each
The errorWhy it is wrongWhat to do instead
Drawing the social cost curve below the supply curve. Social cost is private cost plus external cost, so it is higher at every quantity. Add the external cost to the supply curve at each quantity.
“The efficient amount of pollution is zero.” The first units are worth more than their full cost, harm included. Find where demand meets the social cost curve.
“Any cost to anyone is an externality.” A cost that the buyer or the seller pays is part of the trade. Apply both questions of the third-party test.
“A public good is anything the government provides.” A public good is defined by the good: nonrival and nonexcludable. Ask the rival question and the excludable question.
“One price went up, so there is inflation.” Inflation is a rise in the general level of prices, not in one price. Ask whether prices across the economy rose.
“The economy recovered, so the government's response caused it.” The comparison needed is with what would have happened otherwise, which nobody observes. Ask what the result is being compared with.

Keep the two curves apart. The supply curve shows the producers' own cost. The social cost curve adds the cost to third parties. The market quantity is where demand meets the supply curve. The efficient quantity is where demand meets the social cost curve.

Exam 2 covers Weeks 6–9. The difference between the supply curve and the social cost curve, and the difference between a positive claim and a normative claim, both appear on it.

The main idea of Module 8. A market fails when it produces a quantity other than the efficient quantity. With a negative externality, it produces too much. With a positive externality or a public good, it produces too little. A government can change the incentives with a rule, a tax, tradable permits, or a subsidy, or it can provide the good itself. The whole economy can also fail, with mass unemployment and unstable prices. Whether it recovers on its own was the question the Great Depression raised, and economists still argue about it.

The market quantity counts private costs and benefits. The efficient quantity counts the costs and benefits to everyone.
