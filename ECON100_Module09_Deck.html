What this week covers

For eight weeks, we have asked how a market sets a price and a quantity. This week, we ask a different question about the same economy: who ends up with the income it produces? We measure how income is spread across households, we look at how that spread has changed since 1967, and we look at the gaps between groups.

Day 1: how income is spread out

We start with where income comes from and why households differ. Then we take a town of 20 households, sort them by income, split them into five equal groups, and draw one picture of the whole distribution: the Lorenz curve. We finish with the real numbers for the United States in 2025.

Day 2: over time, and between groups

We compare 1967 with 2025 and ask why the gap widened. Then we look at the differences in income and earnings between racial and ethnic groups and between women and men, and at how economists separate the explanations for those differences.

INCOMEDay 1

Where income comes from: work, ownership, and transfers

Income is the money a household receives in a year. It comes from three sources. A nurse's wages are pay for work. A landlord's rent, a shop owner's profit, and the interest on a savings account are income from owning things. A retiree's Social Security check is a government transfer payment.

Why the source matters this week

Pay for work depends on the wage and the hours. Income from owning things depends on what a household owns, and most households own little that pays income. Transfer payments go mainly to retirees, to people with disabilities, and to households with low incomes. A household's place in the distribution depends on which of these it has.

A household, not a person

The Census Bureau counts income by household: everyone who lives in one housing unit. A household with two earners usually has more income than a household with one, even when the two jobs pay the same. Keep that in mind when you read the numbers.

Income comes from work, from ownership, and from transfers. Most of it is pay for work.
Instructor notes

Ask students for one example of each source from their own household or a household they know. Rent received, a pension, a side business, and a paycheck all come up.

MEASURINGDay 1

Alder Creek: 20 households, sorted and split into fifths

The town of Alder Creek has 20 households. Their incomes, in thousand dollars a year, sorted from lowest to highest, are: 8, 9, 11, 12, 20, 24, 26, 30, 36, 38, 42, 44, 52, 58, 62, 68, 90, 100, 120, and 150. The total is $1,000 thousand a year. A quintile, or fifth, is one-fifth of the households, so each fifth here has 4 households.

Alder Creek: the four households in each fifth, the fifth's total income, and its share of the town's income
Fifth of householdsThe four incomes ($ thousand a year)Income of the fifthShare of the town's income
Lowest fifth8, 9, 11, 12$40 thousand$40 / $1,000 = 4%

Fourth fifth52, 58, 62, 68$240 thousand24%

Reading the table

A fifth's share of income is its income divided by the town's total income. The highest fifth receives 46 percent of the income with 20 percent of the households. The lowest fifth receives 4 percent. The highest fifth's income is $460 thousand / $40 thousand = 11.5 times the lowest fifth's.

The median and the mean

The median household is the one in the middle. In Alder Creek, the middle falls between $38 thousand and $42 thousand, so the median is $40 thousand. The mean is the total divided by 20 households: $50 thousand. The mean is higher because the four highest incomes pull it up. News reports use the median for that reason.

Sort the households, split them into fifths, and give each fifth its share of the income. That table is the first measure of inequality.

MEASURINGDay 1

Build #1: the Lorenz curve for Alder Creek

  1. Draw the axes and label them first. Households, from the lowest income to the highest, go along the horizontal axis as a running total in percent: 20, 40, 60, 80, 100. The share of income they have received together goes up the vertical axis, also as a running total in percent.
  2. The line of equality. If every fifth received 20 percent of the income, the lowest 20 percent of households would have 20 percent, the lowest 40 percent would have 40 percent, and so on. Those points form a straight line from the corner. It is the line of equality.
  3. The first point. The lowest fifth of households received 4 percent of the income. Plot the point at 20 percent of households and 4 percent of income.
  4. Add each fifth to the running total. The lowest two fifths together have 4% + 10% = 14 percent. The lowest three have 14% + 16% = 30 percent. The lowest four have 30% + 24% = 54 percent. All five have 100 percent. Plot each point.
  5. Connect the points. The line from the corner through the five points is the Lorenz curve. It starts at the corner and ends at 100 percent and 100 percent. It bows below the line of equality.
  6. The gap. The farther the Lorenz curve bows away from the line of equality, the more unequal the distribution. The size of the shaded gap is what the Gini index summarizes in one number.
Step 1 of 6

The Lorenz curve plots the cumulative share of income against the cumulative share of households, from the lowest income to the highest. The line of equality is the curve a town would have if every household had the same income.

Each point on the Lorenz curve answers one question: the lowest X percent of households have what percent of the income?
Instructor notes

Draw-it-yourself first: before step 3, ask pairs to plot the first point from the table. The common error is plotting the share of one fifth (10 percent for the second fifth) instead of the running total (14 percent).

At step 6, ask what the curve would look like if one household had all the income: flat along the bottom, then straight up at the end.

MEASURINGDay 1

Reading a Lorenz curve, and the number that summarizes it

Two towns, Birch and Hazel, each have five fifths of households. In Birch, the running totals of income are 7, 19, 36, 60, and 100 percent. In Hazel, they are 3, 10, 22, 45, and 100 percent.

Predict first

In which town is income more unequal, and how would you see it on the two Lorenz curves? Write one sentence before you reveal.

Hazel. At every point, the lowest households in Hazel hold a smaller share of the income than the same households in Birch: 3 percent against 7 percent, 10 against 19, 22 against 36, 45 against 60. Hazel's curve lies below Birch's curve at every point, so it bows farther from the line of equality.

Hazel's highest fifth receives 100% − 45% = 55 percent of the income. Birch's highest fifth receives 100% − 60% = 40 percent.

The Gini index

The Gini index turns the gap between the Lorenz curve and the line of equality into one number between 0 and 1. A Gini of 0 means every household has the same income, so the curve lies on the line. A Gini of 1 means one household has all the income. A larger gap gives a larger Gini. The Census Bureau's Gini index for U.S. households was 0.490 in 2025. We read the number from the Census Bureau. We do not calculate it.

What the curve does not show

The curve shows shares, not dollars. Two towns can have the same curve while one is twice as rich as the other. The curve also does not say why the shares differ, and it does not say whether the shares are fair. Those are separate questions.

Farther from the line of equality means more unequal. The Gini index is that distance as one number.
Instructor notes

HW9a asks students to draw one Lorenz curve in the charter, and HW9b asks them to draw two years on one chart and say which lies farther from the line. This slide is the reading skill both assignments use.

If a student asks how the Gini is calculated, say that it is the shaded area divided by the whole triangle under the line of equality, and that the Census Bureau calculates it from every household, not from five points.

THE DATADay 1

The bottom of the distribution: the poverty line

The poverty line is an income level set by the federal government for each size of family. A family whose income is below the line is counted as poor. In 2025, the line for a family of four was $32,970 a year. The poverty rate is the share of people living below the line: 10.2 percent in 2025, or 34.5 million people.

What the government does

Week 8 ended with the government's role in a market that fails. The government also changes the distribution of income directly. The federal income tax is a progressive tax: households with higher incomes pay a higher share of their income in tax. Transfer payments move money to households without a sale in return: Social Security, unemployment insurance, food assistance, and tax credits for working families with low incomes.

What the count leaves out

The official poverty count uses money income before taxes. It does not count food assistance, housing assistance, or tax credits, which reach many of the same households. A second measure, the Supplemental Poverty Measure, counts them. It also subtracts taxes, work expenses, and medical costs, and it uses a line that changes with the cost of housing where a family lives. That measure gave a poverty rate of 13.1 percent for 2025.

After taxes and tax credits

Taxes and transfers move the whole distribution. The median household income after taxes and tax credits was $76,060 in 2025, against $87,460 before. The federal income tax takes a larger share of higher incomes, and tax credits add to lower incomes.

The poverty line marks the bottom of the distribution. Taxes and transfers change the distribution the market produced.
Instructor notes

One slide only. Week 13 covers fiscal policy. The point here is the connection to Week 8: the government's role includes moving income, not only fixing a market.

Sources: Census Bureau, Poverty in the United States: 2025 (the official measure and the Supplemental Poverty Measure), and Income in the United States: 2025 (post-tax income).

LIMITSDay 1

What a one-year table leaves out

The quintile table describes one year. Three things the table cannot show change what the table means.

Age

Income rises with age and falls after retirement. In 2025, households headed by someone aged 45–54 had a median income of $120,100. Households headed by someone aged 15–24 had $60,870, and those headed by someone 65 or older had $59,680. Part of the spread in any one year is the same people at different ages.

Mobility

Income mobility is movement between the fifths over a lifetime. A student in the lowest fifth this year may be in the fourth fifth in twenty years. Two countries with the same table in one year differ if people move between the fifths in one and stay put in the other.

Household size and benefits

A household of one and a household of five with the same income do not live the same way. The table also counts money income before taxes, so it leaves out the taxes paid and the noncash benefits received.

Watch for: reading the lowest fifth as a fixed group of people. It is a fixed share of households, 20 percent, and the people in it change from year to year.

The table measures one year. Age, mobility, household size, and taxes all change what the table means.
Instructor notes

Ask the class which fifth most of them are in now and which fifth they expect to be in at 45. The point lands without a lecture.

HISTORYDay 1

Where the curve came from: Max Lorenz, 1905

📖 The curve

Max Lorenz published the curve in 1905, in a short article on how to measure the concentration of wealth, while he was a graduate student at the University of Wisconsin. He wanted a picture that would let a reader compare two distributions at a glance instead of comparing two long tables.

📊 The number

Corrado Gini, an Italian statistician, proposed the summary number in 1912. The two tools are still the standard ones. The Census Bureau has published the Gini index for U.S. households every year since 1967, and the World Bank publishes it for most countries.

Checkpoint — positive or normative?

Two statements: A. “In 2025, the highest fifth of households received 52.4 percent of all household income.” B. “The highest fifth receives too large a share.” Which one can the data settle?

Statement A is a positive claim. It can be checked against the Census Bureau's table, and it is either right or wrong.

Statement B is a normative claim. “Too large” depends on a judgment about what the share should be. The data can inform that judgment, but they cannot make it.

The Lorenz curve and the Gini index measure a distribution. They do not judge it.
Instructor notes

One minute on Lorenz. The point is that the tool is older than every current argument about inequality.

OVER TIMEDay 2

Inequality over time: 1967 and 2025 on one chart

The Census Bureau's table goes back to 1967. In 1967, the five fifths received 4.0, 10.8, 17.3, 24.2, and 43.6 percent of household income. In 2025, they received 3.0, 8.2, 13.9, 22.5, and 52.4 percent. The two Lorenz curves put those ten numbers in one picture.

What moved

The highest fifth's share rose. Every other fifth's share fell, the lowest fifth's included. The Gini index rose from 0.397 to 0.490.

What did not move

Every fifth still has 20 percent of the households. The curves show how the income was shared, not how much of it there was.

The 2025 curve lies farther from the line of equality than the 1967 curve at every point. Income is more unequal than it was.

OVER TIMEDay 2

When it changed: the top and bottom fifths, 1967 to 2025

Checkpoint — commit before you reveal

Read the left chart. In which period did the highest fifth's share rise the most: 1967 to 1980, 1980 to 2000, or 2000 to 2025?

1980 to 2000. The share rose from 44.1 percent in 1980 to 49.8 percent in 2000, a rise of 5.7 percentage points. From 1967 to 1980, it rose 0.5 points. From 2000 to 2025, it rose 2.6 points.

Part of the 1980–2000 rise comes from a change in how the data were collected. In 1993, the Census Bureau moved the survey from paper to computer-assisted interviews. It also raised the limits on the highest incomes it records. The highest fifth’s share rose from 46.9 percent in 1992 to 48.9 percent in 1993, and part of that 2.0-point rise comes from the new method. Even with the whole 1993 rise set aside, the 1980–2000 rise is the largest of the three.

The shares were steady until about 1980. The widening came mainly between 1980 and 2000, and it has continued since.
Instructor notes

A change in a share is measured in percentage points, as in Week 8. Say it again here.

Census Bureau footnotes to Table H-2 mark method changes in several years. The largest is 1993: computer-assisted interviewing and higher limits on recorded top incomes. The highest fifth’s share was 46.9 percent in 1992 and 48.9 percent in 1993. The Census Bureau’s 1993 figure with incomes held to the 1992 limits is 48.2 percent. The changes in 2013 and 2017 are smaller. The direction of the series does not depend on any of them.

HISTORYDay 2

The longer story: a century of the top 10 percent

The Census table starts in 1967. Economists Thomas Piketty and Emmanuel Saez used income tax records to extend the series back to 1913. Their series follows the share of all income received by the 10 percent of families with the highest incomes.

Before 1940

From the mid-1920s to 1940, the top 10 percent received about 45 percent of all income.

1940s to 1970s

The share fell during World War II and stayed near 33 percent for three decades. Economists call this the great compression. Wartime wage controls, strong unions, high top tax rates, and the spread of high school and college education are the explanations most often given for it.

Since 1980

The share rose again after 1980 and reached 50.5 percent in 2015, above its 1920s level. The next slide lists the explanations economists test for this rise.

Over a century, the top share was high, then fell for three decades, then rose again. The rise since 1980 took it above its 1920s level.
Instructor notes

The Piketty and Saez series counts income before taxes and transfers from tax returns, so its level is not the same as the Census household share. The shape of the story is what matters here, not the level.

SOURCES OF INEQUALITYDay 2

Why the gap widened after 1980: five explanations

No single cause explains the widening. Economists test five explanations, and the evidence supports each one to some degree. They work at the same time.

ExplanationHow it worksWhat the evidence shows
Technology that pays for skillComputers and automation raised the demand for workers with more education and reduced the demand for routine work. Women who work full time earn about 82 to 84 percent of what men earn. The ratio has risen since 1980.

Sources: BLS CPS Table 37, 2025 annual averages (11-month averages that exclude October 2025); Census P60-289 Table 12.

The within-group ratios are higher for Black and Hispanic workers than for White and Asian workers. Ask why that might be before the next slide: the men's medians differ more between groups than the women's medians do.

BETWEEN GROUPSDay 2

What explains a gap, and what is left over

A gap between two groups is a positive fact. Explaining it takes more than the gap. Economists compare workers in the two groups who are alike in the things that are known to affect pay, and they measure how much of the gap remains.

The explanations that are counted first

  • Education: years of schooling and the degree held
  • Experience: years in the labor force, and time out of it
  • Occupation and industry: which jobs each group holds, and what those jobs pay
  • Hours: full-time against part-time, and overtime
  • Region: where each group lives and what jobs pay there

What is left over

After those differences are counted, part of each gap remains. Labor market discrimination is paying a person less, or treating a person worse on the job, than an equally qualified person because of race, sex, or another characteristic. The part of the gap that remains is the part that discrimination in pay could explain. It also includes differences the data do not measure, so it may overstate or understate discrimination in pay.

Discrimination can also act before the job: in who gets hired, who gets promoted, and who had the chance to get the education. Those effects show up inside the “explanations counted first,” not in what is left over.

Studies that send employers identical résumés with different names measure discrimination in hiring directly. Bertrand and Mullainathan (2004) and Kline, Rose, and Walters (2022) found that résumés with names associated with Black applicants received fewer callbacks.

Checkpoint — commit before you reveal

In 2025, women working full time earned 82 percent of what men earned. Does that number, by itself, measure discrimination in pay?

No. The 82 percent is a ratio of two medians. It includes differences in occupation, hours, and experience. The part that remains after those are counted is smaller than the raw gap, and economists disagree about how much of the remainder is discrimination and how much is differences the data do not capture.

The remainder is not zero. Blau and Kahn (2017) found that about 38 percent of the gap between women’s and men’s pay in 2010 remained after they counted differences in education, experience, occupation, industry, and other measured factors.

A raw gap is a fact. What explains it is measured by comparing like with like, and what remains is where discrimination can be.
Instructor notes

Two federal laws to name: the Equal Pay Act of 1963 and Title VII of the Civil Rights Act of 1964, which made pay discrimination by race, color, religion, sex, or national origin illegal.

Present this slide evenly. The two errors to head off are “the whole gap is discrimination” and “none of it is.”

LIMITSDay 2

What the numbers settle, and the argument they do not

The numbers settle how income is shared, how that changed, and how the groups differ. Whether the government should change the distribution is an argument about values, and the two sides of it are set out below as each side makes them.

The case for and the case against government action to reduce inequality, as each side makes it
QuestionThe case for more actionThe case against more action
FairnessA wide gap between rich and poor in one society is unfair.Taking income from people who earned it is unfair.
GrowthWide gaps leave talent unused and reduce support for open markets.Large rewards for success give people a reason to work, save, and invent.
PoliticsWide gaps give the top too much influence over the rules.Programs that move income invite groups to lobby for a share of them.
The dataThe official tables leave out the highest incomes and understate the gap.The official tables leave out noncash benefits and mobility and overstate it.
Checkpoint — the one to get right

Two statements: A. “In 2025, the median Black household income was 62 percent of the median non-Hispanic White household income.” B. “Congress should raise taxes on the highest fifth to reduce inequality.” Which statement can the data settle?

Statement A is a positive claim. It can be checked against the Census Bureau's table, and it is either right or wrong.

Statement B is a normative claim. It depends on judgments about fairness, incentives, and the role of government. The data can say what a tax would collect and whom it would reach. They cannot say whether Congress should pass it.

Economics measures the distribution and tests the explanations. Choosing what the distribution should be takes a judgment the numbers do not make.

WATCH FORWrap-up

Six common errors this week

Common Module 9 errors and the correction for each
The errorWhy it is wrongWhat to do instead
Plotting a fifth's own share on the Lorenz curve. The curve plots running totals. The second point is the lowest two fifths together. Add each fifth's share to the total so far before you plot.
“The highest fifth has 52 percent of the households.” Every fifth has 20 percent of the households. The 52 percent is a share of the income. Say which thing is being shared: households or income.
Reporting a change in a share in percent instead of percentage points. A rise from 30.0 percent to 36.0 percent is a rise of 6.0 percentage points, not 20 percent. Subtract the two shares and say “percentage points.”
Treating the lowest fifth as a fixed group of people. It is a fixed share of households, and the people in it change with age and circumstances. Remember mobility and age when you read a one-year table.
Reading a raw gap between two groups as a measure of discrimination. The raw gap includes measured differences in education, occupation, hours, and experience. Ask what remains after those differences are counted.
“The data show that inequality is too high.” The data show how high it is. “Too high” is a judgment. Separate the positive claim from the normative claim.

Keep the two axes separate. Households go along the bottom, from the lowest income to the highest, as a running percent. Income goes up the side, as a running percent. Every point answers: the lowest X percent of households have what percent of the income?

The main idea of Module 9. Income comes from work, from ownership, and from transfers, and households differ in income for several reasons at once. Sorting households into fifths and giving each fifth its share of income measures the spread in one table, and the Lorenz curve draws it in one picture. By that measure, U.S. income has become more unequal since 1967, with most of the widening between 1980 and 2000. The groups differ in income and earnings, and economists explain part of each gap and measure what remains. What the spread should be is a normative question that the measurements inform but do not settle.

The table and the curve measure who gets what. The argument about what they should get is a separate one.
