Review: real GDP and the inflation rate

Before you read on, write from memory the measure that economists use for output, the one for jobs, and the one for prices. This slide reviews output and prices. The next slide reviews jobs.

The Week 10 pizza town: nominal GDP and real GDP, with Year 1 as the base year
YearPizzas madePriceNominal GDPReal GDP (Year 1 prices)
1100$10 a pizza$1,000$1,000
2100$12 a pizza$1,200$1,000

Nominal GDP and real GDP

Nominal GDP values each year's output at that year's prices, so it rises when output rises or when prices rise. Real GDP values each year's output at the prices of one base year, so it changes only when output changes. From Year 1 to Year 2, nominal GDP rose 20 percent, but real GDP did not change, because the town made the same 100 pizzas. In a recession, real GDP falls.

The inflation rate

The Consumer Price Index (CPI) compares the cost of a fixed basket of goods with its cost in a base year, where the CPI is 100. The inflation rate is the percent change in the CPI from last year. When the CPI rises from 100.0 to 108.0, the inflation rate is (108.0 − 100.0) / 100.0 = 0.080, or 8.0 percent. The U.S. inflation rate was 1.2 percent in 2020 and 8.0 percent in 2022.

Checkpoint — commit before you reveal

A country's nominal GDP rose 6 percent last year, and its inflation rate was 6 percent. About how much did its real GDP change?

About zero. The whole rise in nominal GDP came from higher prices, so the country produced about the same amount of goods and services. Real GDP measures the change in output alone.

Real GDP measures output, and the inflation rate measures how fast prices rise.

REVIEWDay 1

Review: the unemployment rate and its three types

A person is unemployed when the person has no job, is available to work, and looked for work in the past four weeks. The labor force is the employed plus the unemployed. The unemployment rate is the unemployed divided by the labor force, times 100.

The Week 10 town: 2,760 people employed and 240 people unemployed
MeasureCalculationResult
Labor force2,760 employed + 240 unemployed3,000 people
Unemployment rate240 people / 3,000 people = 0.0808.0 percent

Frictional

Frictional unemployment is people between jobs while they search for a good match.

Structural

Structural unemployment is a mismatch between workers' skills or locations and the jobs available.

Cyclical

Cyclical unemployment is job loss because the whole economy is in a recession. It rises in recessions and falls in expansions.

The natural rate of unemployment is the unemployment rate when only frictional and structural unemployment remain. Economists estimate that the U.S. natural rate has been about 4 to 5 percent in recent decades. Day 2 uses this idea.

Checkpoint — commit before you reveal

Which type of unemployment rises in a recession?

Cyclical unemployment. Frictional and structural unemployment remain even in a strong economy, because people are always changing jobs and the skills that employers want are always changing.

Divide the unemployed by the labor force. Cyclical unemployment is the type that rises in a recession.
THE WHOLE ECONOMYDay 1

Why the whole economy can fall short

In Week 8, we saw that U.S. unemployment stayed above 14 percent from 1931 through 1940. Workers wanted jobs, and factories stood idle. So why did firms not hire the idle workers to make goods?

Picture a town where each business sells to the people who work at the other businesses. A furniture maker hires more workers only if it expects to sell more tables. It expects to sell more tables only if the other businesses are hiring and their workers have pay to spend.

Spending is someone's income

Every dollar that one person spends is a dollar of income for someone else. When households and firms cut their spending, the businesses they bought from earn less. Those businesses then have a reason to cut their own spending and hiring.

Expectations

Each business decides how much to produce from what it expects to sell. If every business expects the others to cut back, each one has a reason to cut back too. The expectation can come true because every business acts on it.

This was John Maynard Keynes's main point in 1936. Below potential GDP, total spending decides how much the economy produces. When total spending falls, firms produce less and hire fewer workers, even though the workers and the factories are still there.

Checkpoint — commit before you reveal

Every business in a town expects sales to fall next year, so each one cuts its hiring now. What happens to the town's total spending, and does that support the businesses' expectation?

Total spending falls, and the expectation comes true. The workers who are not hired have less pay to spend, so every business sells less. No single business can fix the problem, because one business that hires more cannot raise everyone's sales by itself. This is a coordination problem.

Below potential GDP, total spending decides how much the whole economy produces.
Instructor notes

This is the coordination problem that the Week 8 Keynes card raised. The next slide gives total spending its name.

DEMANDDay 1

Aggregate demand: total spending on what the economy produces

Aggregate demand (AD) is the total quantity of final goods and services that households, firms, the government, and buyers in other countries want to buy at each price level. Lakeland, a course-written country, is our example.

The four parts of aggregate demand in Lakeland, in billions of dollars a year, at a price level of 100
PartWho spends, and on whatLakeland
Consumption (C)Households buy groceries, haircuts, and cars and pay rent.$650 billion
Investment (I)Firms buy new buildings, equipment, and software. New homes also count here.$150 billion
Government purchases (G)Governments buy new schools and roads and pay public workers.$170 billion
Net exports (NX)Exports, which buyers abroad buy from us, minus imports, which we buy from abroad.$30 billion
Total spendingC + I + G + NX$1,000 billion

The price level is an index of the prices of all final goods and services in the economy. Like the CPI, it is 100 in the base year. A price level of 110 means that prices are 10 percent above the base year.

These four parts are the four kinds of spending on the goods and services that GDP counts. In Week 2, demand was the quantity of one good that buyers want at each price. Aggregate demand is the quantity of everything the economy produces that buyers want at each price level.

Aggregate demand adds up the spending of households, firms, the government, and buyers abroad.
Instructor notes

Lakeland's parts are illustrative. In the U.S., consumption is about two-thirds of GDP, and net exports are usually negative.

DEMANDDay 1

Build #1: the aggregate demand curve for Lakeland

At price levels of 80, 90, 100, 110, and 120, Lakeland's buyers want $1,200, $1,100, $1,000, $900, and $800 billion of real GDP.

  1. Draw the axes and label them first. Real GDP goes along the horizontal axis, in billions of dollars a year. The price level goes up the vertical axis. These are the economy-wide versions of quantity and price from Week 2.
  2. Plot the schedule. At a price level of 80, buyers want $1,200 billion of real GDP. At 120, they want $800 billion. Plot all five points.
  3. Connect the points. The line is the aggregate demand curve. It slopes downward: at a higher price level, buyers want less real GDP.
  4. A movement along the curve. When the price level rises from 100 to 110, the quantity of real GDP demanded falls from $1,000 billion to $900 billion. The economy moves along the AD curve, and the curve itself does not shift.
Step 1 of 4

Why the AD curve slopes downward. When the price level is higher, the dollars that households and firms hold buy less. With less buying power, they buy fewer goods and services, so the quantity of real GDP demanded is lower.

A higher price level means a lower quantity of real GDP demanded. That is a movement along AD.
Instructor notes

The common error is to explain the slope with one good's price compared with other goods. All prices rise together here, so the reason is the buying power of money.

DEMANDDay 1

What shifts aggregate demand

The rule is the same as in Week 2. A change in the price level moves the economy along the AD curve. Anything else that changes spending at every price level shifts the whole curve.

Events that shift aggregate demand, by the part of spending they change
PartAD shifts right when…AD shifts left when…
Consumptionhouseholds grow more confident about their jobs, or home prices and stock prices risehouseholds grow worried about their jobs, or home prices and stock prices fall
Investmentfirms expect higher sales and build new plantsfirms expect lower sales and put off new equipment
Government purchasesgovernments buy more roads, schools, and equipmentgovernments cut their purchases
Net exportsbuyers in other countries buy more of our goodsbuyers in other countries buy fewer of our goods
Checkpoint — commit before you reveal

Business owners across Lakeland expect sales to fall next year, so they put off buying new equipment. Does AD shift, and which way?

AD shifts left. Investment falls at every price level, so total spending falls at every price level. Nothing has happened to the price level itself, so this is a shift of the curve, not a movement along it.

A change in the price level moves along AD. A change in C, I, G, or NX for any other reason shifts AD.
HISTORYDay 1

History: Say's law and Keynes's law

In Week 8, we met two answers to the question of whether an economy recovers on its own. This week's model gives each answer a place.

📖 Say's law, 1803

The French economist Jean-Baptiste Say argued that producing goods creates the income to buy them. Say's law is often summarized as “supply creates its own demand.” On this view, total spending is always enough to buy what the economy can produce, and the economy returns to full employment on its own.

📖 Keynes's law, 1936

John Maynard Keynes argued that, in the short run, firms produce what buyers will purchase. Keynes's law is often summarized as “demand creates its own supply.” On this view, a fall in total spending lowers output and employment, and the economy can stay below full employment for years.

Checkpoint — commit before you reveal

From 1931 through 1940, U.S. unemployment stayed above 14 percent. Which law does that decade fit better, and why?

Keynes's law. Total spending fell, and output and employment stayed low for a decade instead of returning to full employment on their own. Many economists find that Say's law fits better over long periods when the economy is near full employment. Day 2 shows where each law fits on the aggregate supply curve.

Say argued that output sets spending. Keynes argued that, in the short run, spending sets output.
Instructor notes

Present both views evenly. Say's law describes the long run well in many periods. Keynes was answering the 1930s.

SUPPLYDay 2

Aggregate supply: what firms produce at each price level

Aggregate supply (AS) is the total quantity of final goods and services that the firms in an economy produce at each price level. At price levels of 70, 80, 90, and 100, Lakeland's firms produce $700, $800, $900, and $1,000 billion of real GDP. At 110 and 120, they still produce $1,000 billion.

The rising part

Many of a firm's costs, such as wages, are set in advance and change slowly. When the prices that firms sell at rise faster than their costs, producing more is profitable, so firms hire more workers and produce more. Below $1,000 billion, a higher price level brings more real GDP.

The vertical part

At $1,000 billion, Lakeland is using its workers and machines at their normal full rates, and unemployment is at its natural rate. A higher price level cannot bring more output, so the curve turns vertical.

Potential GDP is the real GDP that an economy produces when its workers, machines, and technology are fully used at normal rates, with unemployment at its natural rate. In Lakeland, potential GDP is $1,000 billion.

The AS curve rises until real GDP reaches potential GDP. There, it turns vertical.
Instructor notes

This course uses one AS curve, with no separate short-run and long-run curves. Week 14 returns to how quickly an economy adjusts.

SUPPLYDay 2

Build #2: the AS curve and the PPC

  1. The PPC from Week 1. At a point on the curve, the economy's workers and machines are fully used at normal rates. At a point inside the curve, some of them are idle. A point outside the curve is out of reach with today's resources and technology.
  2. Lakeland's AS curve. Real GDP goes across, and the price level goes up. The curve rises and then turns vertical.
  3. Potential GDP. The vertical part of the AS curve is at potential GDP, $1,000 billion. It is the most the economy can produce when its workers and machines are fully used at normal rates.
  4. Point R is inside the PPC. Some workers and machines are idle. On the AS curve, R is on the rising part, below potential GDP, at $800 billion. Unemployment is above its natural rate, and the extra is cyclical unemployment.
  5. Point C is on the PPC. Workers and machines are fully used at normal rates. On the AS curve, C is where the curve turns vertical, at potential GDP. Unemployment is at its natural rate.
  6. Point N is outside the PPC. No point on the AS curve matches N, because the AS curve does not go to the right of potential GDP. To reach N, the economy needs more workers, more machines, or better technology. Those changes shift the PPC outward and move potential GDP to the right, which Week 16 covers.
Step 1 of 6
The rising part of AS is inside the PPC. The vertical part is on the PPC. No point on AS is outside it.
Instructor notes

The PPC shows two goods and the AS curve shows all output as one number, so the match is between positions, not between exact numbers.

EQUILIBRIUMDay 2

Macroeconomic equilibrium: where AD crosses AS

Macroeconomic equilibrium is the price level and real GDP where the AD curve crosses the AS curve. At that price level, the quantity of real GDP that buyers want equals the quantity that firms produce.

Lakeland's AD and AS schedules, in billions of dollars a year
Price levelADAS
80$1,200$800
90$1,100$900
100$1,000$1,000
110$900$1,000
120$800$1,000

Above 100, firms produce more than buyers want, and firms have unsold goods, so firms have a reason to lower their prices. As prices fall, buyers want more, and production stays at $1,000 billion. Below 100, buyers want more than firms produce, so firms have a reason to raise prices and produce more. At 100, the two quantities are equal.

Checkpoint — commit before you reveal

Lakeland's equilibrium is at potential GDP. How does its unemployment rate compare with its natural rate of unemployment?

The two are equal. At potential GDP, only frictional and structural unemployment remain. There is no cyclical unemployment.

Equilibrium is where total spending equals total production: a price level of 100 and $1,000 billion of real GDP.
Instructor notes

This is the Week 4 surplus-and-shortage reasoning applied to all output at once.

SHOCKSDay 2

A demand shock: total spending falls

A demand shock is an event that shifts the AD curve. Suppose households in Lakeland grow worried about their jobs, and firms put off buying new equipment. Total spending falls at every price level, so AD shifts left.

What the graph shows

The new crossing is lower and to the left. The price level falls, and real GDP falls. Firms produce less, so they need fewer workers, and cyclical unemployment rises. The economy has moved inside its PPC.

In practice, prices rarely fall. A demand shock more often slows inflation. The 2008–09 recession followed a fall in home prices, which cut the spending of households and home builders.

Checkpoint — commit before you reveal

Suppose total spending rises instead, when Lakeland is already at potential GDP. What happens to the price level and to real GDP?

The price level rises, and real GDP does not change. AD shifts right and crosses the vertical part of AS. The economy cannot produce more than potential GDP, so the extra spending raises only prices. That is inflation with no gain in output.

Where Say's law and Keynes's law fit. On the rising part of AS, a shift in AD changes real GDP. That fits Keynes's law. On the vertical part, output is set by the economy's workers, machines, and technology, and a shift in AD changes only the price level. That fits Say's law.

Below potential GDP, a demand shock moves the price level and real GDP in the same direction. At potential GDP, more spending raises only the price level.
SHOCKSDay 2

A supply shock: the price of oil rises

A supply shock is an event that shifts the AS curve. Suppose the price of oil rises sharply. Oil is used to make and move almost everything, so costs rise for nearly every firm at once. At each price level on the rising part of AS, firms produce less than before, so the rising part of the AS curve shifts left.

What the graph shows

The new crossing is higher and to the left. The price level rises, and real GDP falls. Firms produce less and need fewer workers, so unemployment rises. Potential GDP does not change, because Lakeland still has the same workers, machines, and technology.

Prices and unemployment rise together. Stagflation is high inflation together with rising unemployment and stagnant or falling output.

The 1970s

In October 1973, Arab oil-exporting countries stopped oil shipments to the United States, and the price of oil rose to about four times its earlier level. Inflation rose from 6.2 percent in 1973 to 11.0 percent in 1974. Unemployment rose from 4.9 percent in 1973 to 8.5 percent in 1975.

Checkpoint — commit before you reveal

In most recessions, inflation slows. How can one event raise prices and unemployment at the same time?

A rise in costs shifts the AS curve left. Along the AD curve, the new crossing is at a higher price level and a lower real GDP. Lower output means fewer jobs, so unemployment rises while prices rise. That is what happened after the 1973 oil shock.

A supply shock moves the price level and real GDP in opposite directions. That is how a rise in the price of oil caused stagflation.

Checkpoint — commit before you reveal

In 2009, U.S. consumer prices fell 0.4 percent, and the unemployment rate rose from 5.8 percent in 2008 to 9.3 percent. Was 2009 mainly a demand shock or a supply shock?

A demand shock. Prices fell while unemployment rose, so prices and output both fell. That is the pattern of AD shifting left. Falling home prices and the financial crisis cut the spending of households and firms.

Same direction: a shift in AD. Opposite directions: a shift in AS.

POLICYDay 2

After a supply shock: the policymaker's choice

After the oil shock, Lakeland's price level is higher and its real GDP is lower than before. Governments and central banks can shift AD with tools that Week 13 covers. This slide looks only at the choice they face.

Raise spending

AD shifts right. Real GDP rises toward potential GDP, and cyclical unemployment falls. But the price level rises further.

Cut spending

AD shifts left. The price level comes back down. But real GDP falls further, and unemployment rises further.

The model shows the tradeoff, but it does not say which choice is better. That depends on how much weight a person gives to higher prices compared with lost jobs.

Checkpoint — positive or normative?

Statement A: “If Lakeland raises spending after the oil shock, its price level will rise.” Statement B: “Lakeland should accept higher prices to protect jobs.” Which statement is positive, and which is normative?

A is positive, and B is normative. Statement A is a prediction that evidence can check. Statement B weighs higher prices against lost jobs, and that weighing is a value judgment.

After a supply shock, more spending brings back output but raises prices further. Less spending holds prices down but costs more output and jobs.
Instructor notes

Keep the tools out of this slide. Week 13 covers fiscal and monetary policy.

WATCH FORWrap-up

Six common errors this week

Common Module 11 errors and the correction for each
The errorWhy it is wrongWhat to do instead
Calling a change in the price level a shift of AD. A change in the price level moves the economy along the AD curve. Shift AD only for a change in spending at every price level.
Reading the vertical axis as the price of one good. The price level is an index of all prices in the economy. Read it like the CPI: 100 in the base year.
Shifting AD for a rise in the price of oil. Oil raises firms' costs, which changes how much firms produce at each price level. Shift AS left for a rise in costs.
Moving real GDP past potential GDP when spending rises. The AS curve is vertical at potential GDP. At potential GDP, more spending raises only the price level.
Reading a price level of 110 as 110 percent inflation. 110 means prices are 10 percent above the base year. Calculate inflation as the percent change from last year.
“The model says the government should raise spending.” The model shows the tradeoff. Choosing between prices and jobs is a value judgment. Separate the positive prediction from the normative choice.

Before you shift a curve, ask whether the event changes spending or changes costs. A change in spending shifts AD. A change in firms' costs shifts AS.

Exam 3 covers Weeks 10–12, in class, on paper. From this week: reading equilibrium from AD and AS, deciding which curve an event shifts, and telling a demand shock from a supply shock.

The main idea of Module 11. Aggregate demand is the total spending of households, firms, the government, and buyers abroad, and it slopes downward against the price level. Aggregate supply is the total output of firms. It rises until real GDP reaches potential GDP, where the economy's workers and machines are fully used, and there it turns vertical. Where the two curves cross, the model gives the price level and real GDP. A demand shock moves prices and output in the same direction. A supply shock moves them in opposite directions, which is how a rise in the price of oil produced the stagflation of the 1970s. The model shows the tradeoffs a policymaker faces, and choosing among them is a normative question.

AD is total spending, AS is total production, and a shift in either one moves prices, output, and jobs.
