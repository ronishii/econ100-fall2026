What this week covers

Last week, every seller was a price taker. This week, we look at markets in which a seller can set its own price. A seller with that power faces a demand curve of its own, and that one change is behind most of what follows in the module. Almost everything you buy comes from one of these markets.

Day 1: monopoly and monopolistic competition

We place four market structures on one line. Then we study monopoly by following one coffee roaster that is the only seller in its town. We find how many bags it sells and at what price, and we measure what its market power costs the town. We finish with monopolistic competition, the structure most small businesses are in.

Day 2: oligopoly

We study oligopoly, a market with only a few sellers, by following two roasters that share one town. Each one's best choice depends on what the other does. We read a payoff table, find what each roaster does, and see why an agreement between them to keep prices high does not hold. We end with the law Congress passed about such agreements.

FOUR STRUCTURESDay 1

Four market structures on one line

Four questions place any market on this line. How many sellers are there? Is the product the same from each seller? Can a new seller enter? Can any one seller change the price?

A seller has market power when it can raise its price without losing every buyer. A seller with market power is a price setter. The three structures to the right of perfect competition are imperfectly competitive, because at least one of last week's four conditions fails in each of them.

Watch for: hearing “monopoly” and thinking it means “big.” A monopoly is the only seller of a product that has no close substitute. A town's water utility is a monopoly. The largest company you can name almost certainly is not.

Instructor notes

State each of the four questions and ask for a market that gives the answer the structure needs. Then place the three markets from the brain dump on the line by asking the four questions out loud. At least one will be arguable. Say so instead of forcing it; the arguable ones show that the line is a line and not four bins.

Say the watch-for. “Big” and “monopoly” are different words, and the quiz tests the difference twice.

MONOPOLYDay 1

Monopoly: the seller's own demand curve slopes down

This slide and the next four slides are about monopoly. A monopoly is the only seller of a product that has no close substitute.

Our example is one coffee roaster. It is the only seller of freshly roasted coffee in its town, and buyers in the town have no close substitute for it. So the roaster is a monopoly, and the whole town's demand for bags of coffee is the roaster's own demand. Last week, one farm's demand curve was flat. This week, the monopoly roaster's demand curve slopes down.

Why the roaster's demand slopes down

The roaster is the only seller, so its demand curve is the town's demand curve for roasted coffee. Like any market demand curve, it slopes down. When the roaster raises its price, some buyers buy less coffee or stop buying it, but the rest stay. The roaster can sell 4 thousand bags a month at $6 a bag, or 6 thousand bags at $4 a bag.

What the roaster now has to decide

The roaster chooses a point on its demand curve. A higher price sells fewer bags. A lower price sells more bags, but the lower price applies to every bag it sells, including the bags it was already selling at the higher price.

Watch for: “A monopoly can charge whatever it wants.” It cannot. The demand curve is still there, and buyers still leave when the price rises. What the monopoly has is a choice of a point on that curve, and every point has a cost.

Instructor notes

Name the structure first: the roaster is a monopoly, and slides 7–11 stay with it. Monopolistic competition starts on the sandwich-shop slide, and oligopoly starts on Day 2.

Put both graphs up and ask what changed. The answer is that the roaster's demand curve slopes. Most of the module follows from that change, and it is worth saying so out loud.

Work the right panel in dollars. At $6, the roaster sells 4 thousand bags. To sell 6 thousand, it has to drop the price to $4. Ask what that costs. The answer students miss is that the lower price applies to the 4 thousand bags the roaster was already selling.

We stay with the monopoly roaster. The table shows what the town buys at each price, and what the roaster's total revenue is at each price. Total revenue is price × bags sold. Marginal revenue is the additional revenue from selling one more unit, here one more thousand bags.

Read the column for the 5th thousand bags. To sell 5 thousand bags instead of 4 thousand, the roaster lowers its price from $6 to $5. It gains $5 on the 5th thousand bags. It also gives up $1 on each of the 4 thousand bags it was already selling, which is 4 × $1 = $4. The net gain is $5 − $4 = $1 thousand. Checkpoint — calculate one

What is the marginal revenue of the 3rd thousand bags, and why is it less than the $7 price? Use the total revenue row.

$5 thousand. Total revenue rises from $16 thousand at 2 thousand bags to $21 thousand at 3 thousand bags, and $21 − $16 = $5. The 3rd thousand bags sell for $7 each, but the price on the first 2 thousand bags fell from $8 to $7, which gives up $2 thousand. $7 − $2 = $5.

For a monopoly, and for any other price setter, marginal revenue is less than the price, because the lower price applies to every unit.

MONOPOLYDay 1

Build #1: how many bags the monopoly sells, and at what price

  1. Draw the axes and label the units first. Bags of coffee per month, in thousands, go on the horizontal axis. Dollars per bag go on the vertical axis.
  2. The roaster's own demand curve. At $10 a bag, nobody buys. Each $1 cut in the price sells another thousand bags. This is the line that was flat last week.
  3. Marginal revenue. This line shows the marginal revenue from the table. It starts at the same point as demand and falls twice as fast, because each extra thousand bags requires a lower price on every bag.
  4. Marginal cost. Roasting costs $2 a bag at any quantity, and there are no other costs. The cost side did not change this week. The demand side did.
  5. Where to stop, and what to charge. The roaster sells the largest quantity at which marginal revenue is at least marginal cost. The 4th thousand bags add $3 thousand of revenue for $2 thousand of cost, so the roaster sells them. The 5th thousand bags add $1 thousand for $2 thousand, so the roaster does not sell them. The roaster sells 4 thousand bags. Then it reads the price from the demand curve, not from the marginal revenue line. Buyers will pay $6 a bag for 4 thousand bags.
Step 1 of 5

A monopoly, like any price setter, finds its profit-maximizing output at the largest quantity at which marginal revenue is at least marginal cost. It then reads its price from the demand curve at that quantity. The monopoly roaster's profit is revenue minus cost: 4 thousand bags × $6 a bag = $24 thousand, minus 4 thousand bags × $2 a bag = $8 thousand, which leaves $16 thousand a month.

The marginal revenue line tells the roaster how much to sell. The demand curve tells it what price to charge.
Instructor notes

Draw-it-yourself first: before step 3, ask pairs to find the best output from the table alone, by reading down the marginal revenue row until it drops below $2.

At step 5, the most common error of the module is reading the price from the marginal revenue line. Point at the marginal revenue line and say that it tells the roaster how much to sell. Then point at the demand curve and say that it tells the roaster what price to charge.

Close on the comparison with last week: a price taker would have sold where price equals marginal cost, which is 8 thousand bags at $2. The next slide is what that difference costs.

MONOPOLYDay 1

What the monopoly costs the town: half the output, three times the price

A competitive market would sell where the price equals marginal cost: 8 thousand bags a month at $2 a bag. The monopoly roaster sells 4 thousand bags at $6 a bag. The figure measures the difference.

Buyers who still buy

They pay $6 a bag instead of $2 a bag. The extra $4 a bag on 4 thousand bags, which is $16 thousand a month, is transferred from buyers to the roaster.

Buyers who stop buying

4 thousand bags that buyers valued above the $2 it costs to roast them are not made. Nobody gets those bags, and nobody earns anything from them. That lost value is deadweight loss, $8 thousand a month.

The roaster

It earns $16 thousand a month above its roasting costs. A competitive market would have left it nothing above cost. That gain is the reason it sets the price where it does.

Checkpoint — commit before you reveal

The roaster sold fewer bags to raise the price. Is the shaded triangle money that the roaster gained?

No. Nobody gets the triangle. Those bags were not roasted and not sold, so nobody earned anything from them and nobody drank the coffee. The triangle is not a transfer from buyers to the roaster. It is value that no longer exists.

The roaster's gain is the rectangle: the extra $4 a bag on the 4 thousand bags it does sell. All of that money was money that buyers kept when the price was $2 a bag. You met deadweight loss in Week 4, under price controls. Here, the restricted output does the same thing that a price control did.

Instructor notes

Take the three cards in order. The middle card needs the most time. Ask who those buyers are and let the class describe them.

Expect a strong first answer of “yes, that is the roaster's profit” on the checkpoint. Resolve it with one question: who is holding those bags? Nobody. They were never roasted.

Name deadweight loss once and say that the class met the term in Week 4. Do not develop it further. The callback is the point.

MONOPOLYDay 1

Why the monopoly's profit lasts: barriers to entry

Last week, an economic profit of $36 a week on one farm attracted new farms, and entry pushed the profit to zero. The monopoly roaster earns $16 thousand a month above its costs, and no new roaster appears. A monopoly keeps its profit because something keeps new sellers out. That something is a barrier to entry.

Four kinds of barrier to entry, with an example of each
BarrierWhat it isExample
A legal barrierThe law gives one seller the exclusive right to sell the product for a period of time.A patent on a new medicine gives its maker 20 years as the only seller.
Control of a key inputOne seller owns something that any competitor would need.A company that owns the only quarry of a rare stone in a region.
A very large start-up costA new seller would have to spend so much to begin that the market cannot support another one.A new maker of large commercial aircraft, which would need factories and a design program before it sold a single plane.
A natural monopolyOne seller can supply the whole market at a lower cost per unit than two or more sellers could.A town's water utility. Two sets of pipes would cost more per household, not less.

A barrier to entry is anything that makes it hard for a new firm to start selling in a market. A natural monopoly is a market in which one seller can supply the whole market at a lower cost per unit than two or more sellers could.

Market power lasts only as long as a barrier keeps new sellers out.
Instructor notes

Connect this slide to last week in one sentence: entry is what removed the profit, so a barrier to entry is what preserves it.

The patent row returns on the last Day 2 slide, where the market power is created on purpose and for a reason. Mention that now, and leave it.

MONOPOLISTIC COMPETITIONDay 1

Monopolistic competition: many sellers, all a little different

We now leave monopoly and turn to monopolistic competition. A town has dozens of sandwich shops, and anyone can open one. But the shops are not identical. Each one has its own location, recipes, and service. So each shop has a little market power: raise the price 50 cents, and the shop loses some customers but not all of them.

What entry still does

New shops open while there is an economic profit to be made, and each new shop takes some customers from the others. Each shop's own demand curve shifts to the left until the price just covers average total cost. The economic profit goes to zero, as it did in Week 6.

What entry cannot do

The price stays above marginal cost: $7.00 against $3.00 a sandwich. And the shop settles at 40 sandwiches a day when its average total cost would be lowest at 60. The shop carries 20 sandwiches a day of idle capacity.

Monopolistic competition is a market with many sellers, easy entry, and differentiated products, which means that buyers see the sellers' products as different from one another. In the long run, each seller earns zero economic profit, charges a price above marginal cost, and produces less than the quantity at which its average total cost is lowest.

Monopolistic competition ends with zero economic profit, but the price is still above marginal cost, and output is still below the lowest-cost quantity.
Instructor notes

Start from the brain-dump examples: coffee shops, nail salons, food trucks. This is the structure most of the class works in.

Walk the figure slowly. The demand line touches average total cost and never crosses it. That is what zero economic profit looks like when the demand curve slopes.

The idle capacity is the surprising half. Half-empty restaurants are what product variety costs. Ask whether the class would trade the variety for the efficiency. Take two answers and resolve nothing. The last Day 2 slide takes the question up.

OLIGOPOLYDay 2

Oligopoly: when there are only a few sellers

Day 2 is about oligopoly. So far, each seller has decided on its own. A price taker accepts the market price. A monopoly chooses a point on its demand curve. In an oligopoly, a seller cannot decide alone. With a few large sellers, what each one should do depends on what the others do, and the others are working out the same thing about it.

What makes an oligopoly

An oligopoly is a market with a few large sellers, each of which is large enough that its choices change the others' sales. Entry is hard, usually because of a large start-up cost. Mobile networks, airlines on one route, and the makers of commercial aircraft are examples.

How concentrated a market is

The four-firm concentration ratio is the share of a market's sales held by its four largest sellers. In a regional parcel-delivery market, the four largest firms hold 31% + 24% + 17% + 12% = 84% of sales, and 9 smaller firms share the other 16%. A high ratio says that a few sellers hold most of the market. It does not say how they behave.

How Day 2 works. We follow two roasters that share one town, Northgate and Ridgeway. With only two sellers, their market is an oligopoly. Each month, each one sets a HIGH price or a LOW price without knowing what the other has chosen. We put the four possible results in one table, read it, and find what each roaster does. Then we ask what the law says about an agreement between them.

Instructor notes

If you collected muddiest points on Day 1, answer the top one here. The usual one is why marginal revenue sits below the price. Use the 5th-thousand arithmetic, not the diagram.

OLIGOPOLYDay 2

Oligopoly: two roasters, one town, and the payoff table

Northgate and Ridgeway are the only two roasters in their town, so their market is an oligopoly. A payoff table shows what each player earns in every combination of choices. Northgate picks a row. Ridgeway picks a column. The cell where the row and the column meet shows what each roaster earns that month, in thousands of dollars of profit.

Checkpoint — read one cell

Northgate prices HIGH and Ridgeway prices LOW. What does each roaster earn, and why is the result so uneven?

Northgate earns $10 thousand and Ridgeway earns $80 thousand. Ridgeway's lower price takes most of the town's buyers from Northgate. Ridgeway sells many more bags, and Northgate sells very few.

Both roasters choose at the same time, so neither one knows the other's choice when it decides. That is why each one has to think about both columns.

Read a payoff table one cell at a time: find the row, find the column, and read both numbers where they meet.
Instructor notes

Make sure everyone can read a cell before Build #2. Ask what Northgate earns at HIGH/LOW and take the answer from the class.

Say that both choose at the same time. That is what makes the table a decision and not a negotiation.

OLIGOPOLYDay 2

Build #2: why the oligopoly's agreement breaks

  1. The four outcomes. Northgate picks a row, Ridgeway picks a column, and the cell where they meet shows what each one earns. Both choose at the same time, so neither knows the other's choice.
  2. Suppose Ridgeway prices HIGH. Northgate compares its own two numbers in that column: $60 thousand if it also prices HIGH, and $80 thousand if it prices LOW. Northgate prices LOW.
  3. Now suppose Ridgeway prices LOW. Northgate compares its two numbers in that column: $10 thousand if it holds the HIGH price, and $30 thousand if it matches with LOW. Northgate prices LOW.
  4. So LOW is better for Northgate whatever Ridgeway does. Northgate never has to guess what Ridgeway will do. Ridgeway reads the same table, and the numbers are symmetric, so the same two comparisons give Ridgeway the same answer. Both price LOW.
  5. Where they end up, and where they would rather be. Both earn $30 thousand. Both would have earned $60 thousand under the agreement they made at lunch. Neither roaster was foolish, and neither was cheated. Each did the best thing available to it, and together they did worse.
Step 1 of 5

A dominant strategy is a choice that gives a player a better result than its alternative, whatever the other player does. For both roasters, LOW is a dominant strategy. The outcome in which every player plays its dominant strategy is the equilibrium of the game. A game in which both players have a dominant strategy and both would be better off if both chose the other way is called a prisoner's dilemma.

Hold the other player's choice fixed, and compare your own two numbers. Then do it again for the other column.
Instructor notes

The move to teach is: hold the other player's choice fixed and compare your own two numbers. Do it with your hand covering the other column. Say “whatever Ridgeway does” out loud each time. That phrase states the definition of a dominant strategy.

After step 5, name the three terms. If you ran the game, attach the terms to what a student said about why they priced LOW.

Say the caveat: this is what happens when the roasters choose once. The next slide asks what changes when they choose every month for years.

OLIGOPOLYDay 2

Cartels, and why they are unstable

A cartel is that agreement made formal: several sellers in an oligopoly agree to act as if they were one seller, holding output down and the price up. An agreement among sellers to fix prices or limit output is called collusion, and it is illegal in the United States. It would be fragile even if it were legal.

Why cartels form

Together, the two roasters earn $60 thousand + $60 thousand = $120 thousand a month under the agreement, and $30 thousand + $30 thousand = $60 thousand a month without it. There is real money in cooperating. That is why sellers try.

Why cartels break down

Each member gains $80 thousand − $60 thousand = $20 thousand by quietly breaking the agreement while the other holds the HIGH price. Each member also knows that the other faces the same $20 thousand.

Checkpoint — commit before you reveal

Our two roasters compete every month, for years, and each one can see what the other did last month. Does that change anything?

Yes, a great deal. Breaking the agreement now gains $20 thousand once. It can also cost the $60 thousand cooperative result in every month afterward, because the other roaster can punish the break by pricing LOW from then on. When the future is worth enough, holding the HIGH price becomes the better choice.

That is why real cartels survive at all. It is also why they still fall apart when a member is desperate for cash, when demand drops, or when there are enough members that a secret price cut is hard to detect.

Watch for: concluding that cartels never work. Cartels hold together often enough that the law treats them as a real problem. The accurate claim is narrower: a cartel is unstable, because every member always has a private reason to break it.

Instructor notes

Make the $120 thousand against $60 thousand comparison explicit. A cartel is not irrational. It is worth a lot to its members.

Expect the first answers on the checkpoint to split. The resolving idea is that repetition gives the other side a way to punish a break, and the punishment only matters if the future matters.

Ask what makes a secret price cut harder to detect: more members, secret discounts, custom contracts. Do not develop it. You are setting up the Standard Oil slide.

HISTORYDay 2

What Congress and the Supreme Court did about it

📜 The Sherman Antitrust Act, 1890

Congress passed the Sherman Antitrust Act on July 2, 1890. Its two main sections make two things illegal: agreements that restrain trade, and the act of monopolizing a market. It is the law that made the roasters' agreement a crime. Laws of this kind are called antitrust laws, because the large combinations of companies they were written against were called trusts.

⚖️ Standard Oil, 1911

In Standard Oil Co. of New Jersey v. United States, decided May 15, 1911, the Supreme Court found that the company had violated the Act and ordered it broken into its separate companies. By 1880, Standard Oil controlled the refining of about 90 to 95 percent of the oil produced in the United States.

Checkpoint — the part that is still argued about

During the years in which Standard Oil built that share, the price of kerosene fell. Defenders of the company have pointed to that fact ever since. Does a falling price settle whether the breakup was right?

No. The price fell over time, and that is a different claim from the one the question needs. What nobody can observe is the price that would have prevailed with more refiners competing. The comparison the question needs is with that alternative, not with the past.

Historians and economists still disagree about this case, and they disagree about the evidence, not only about values. Published accounts even differ on how many companies the breakup produced. The Court's own opinion names 37 subsidiaries, and other sources count 33.

A price that fell over time and a price lower than it would otherwise have been are different claims, and only the first one is what the price data show.
Instructor notes

Give the two dates precisely. Students remember 1890 and 1911, and the dates anchor the regulation material in Week 8.

The checkpoint is the module's fact-versus-opinion item, and the quiz tests it. Expect a first answer of “yes, the price fell, so it was fine.” The question that resolves it is: fell compared with what?

Say plainly that well-informed people still disagree about this case. That is what a hard empirical question looks like. Do not resolve it for them.

LIMITSDay 2

Three questions the diagram cannot answer

💰 The mark-up that pays for the idea

A drug patent creates a monopoly on purpose, so that inventing the drug is worth doing. The price above cost is the reward for the invention. Is that market power a failing of the market, or the price of having the medicine at all?

🥪 The variety we are paying for

Monopolistic competition leaves sandwich shops with idle capacity and prices above marginal cost. It also gives a town 40 different kinds of sandwich. Which would you rather have?

🔧 The market that works best with one seller

A town's water supply is a natural monopoly. Two sets of water pipes down the same street would cost more per household, not less. Should there be one seller? And if there is one seller, who decides what it charges? Week 8 takes up that question.

Checkpoint — the one to get right

Two statements about the Day 1 monopoly roaster: A. “With market power, the town gets 4 thousand bags a month instead of 8 thousand.” B. “The town would be better off if the roaster were broken up.” Which statement can the numbers settle?

Statement A is a positive claim. It follows from the demand curve and the marginal cost, and it is either right or wrong.

Statement B is a normative claim. “Better off” needs someone to say better for whom, and weighed how: against the owner's loss, and against whatever the mark-up was paying for. The model measures the size of the effect. It does not say what to do about it.

Economics can measure the trade-off precisely. It cannot make the trade-off for you. Measuring it and deciding what to do about it are two separate steps.
Instructor notes

Three cards, one minute each, with no editorializing. The patent card is the clearest case in which market power is created on purpose and for a good reason.

Present both halves of the checkpoint as evenly as you can. A class that decides the course is against business stops listening, and so does a class that decides the course is for monopoly.

WATCH FORWrap-up

Six common errors this week

Common Module 7 errors and the correction for each
The errorWhy it is wrongWhat to do instead
“A monopoly can charge whatever it wants.” It picks a point on a demand curve. A higher price sells fewer units, and that is true at every price. Say which quantity goes with the price you name.
“Monopoly means a very big company.” It means the only seller of a product with no close substitute. Size is a different question. Ask whether buyers have a close substitute.
“Read the price where marginal revenue meets marginal cost.” That crossing gives the quantity. The price is on the demand curve, straight above that quantity. Find the quantity first, then go up to the demand curve.
“Zero economic profit means the market is perfectly competitive.” Monopolistic competition also ends at zero economic profit, with a mark-up and idle capacity that perfect competition does not have. Check whether the price equals marginal cost.
“They priced LOW because they did not trust each other.” Trust is not required. Pricing LOW pays more whatever the other firm does, which is a stronger and simpler reason. Compare each player's two numbers in each column.
“Market power is bad.” Market power restricts output, and that part is measurable. Whether the result is bad depends on what the market power is paying for, and the diagram does not say. Keep the positive claim and the normative claim in separate sentences.

Keep the two lines apart. Demand tells a price setter what price goes with each quantity. Marginal revenue tells it what one more unit adds to revenue. Two of the errors above come from mixing them up.

Exam 2 covers Weeks 6–9. Module 6 and Module 7 are a large part of it. The difference between price = marginal cost and marginal revenue = marginal cost appears more than once.

The main idea of Module 7. When a seller can set its own price, as a monopoly can, it faces a demand curve of its own, and its marginal revenue is less than its price. A monopoly sells less than a competitive market would, at a higher price, and the difference is a deadweight loss. In an oligopoly, a few sellers share a market, and each one's best choice depends on the others. In that market, and an agreement to keep prices high is unstable, because each seller gains by breaking it.

A monopoly sells less at a higher price. In an oligopoly, a few sellers who agree on a high price each have a reason to break the agreement.
