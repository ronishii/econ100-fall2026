§3.1Day 1 · Tuesday

A market is just buyers and sellers, meeting

A market is any arrangement that brings buyers and sellers together. It does not need a building, a bell, or a floor full of people shouting. The Palomar parking lot at 9:15 is a market for parking spaces. Your phone is a market for about forty things at once.

🛍️ The buyers’ side — DEMAND

How much buyers are willing and able to purchase at each price. That is this week, all of it.

Willing and able matters. Wanting a Tesla is not demand. Wanting a Tesla with the money to buy one is.

🏭 The sellers’ side — SUPPLY

How much sellers are willing to produce and offer at each price. That is Week 3.

Week 4 is where the two sides meet and settle on a price. We are deliberately taking one side at a time.

⚠️ Watch out for: “demand” in ordinary English means wanting something. In economics it means wanting it and being able to pay for it, at a stated price. A sentence like “demand for housing is huge” is almost meaningless to an economist until you say at what price.

Every market has two sides. This week we look only at the buyers’ side.

§3.1Day 1 · Tuesday

Willingness to pay: the number already in your head

Right now, without doing any arithmetic, there is a number in your head for a large coffee from the cart outside MD. Somewhere there is a price at which you say yes, fine — and a price one dollar higher where you say no, I’ll make it at home. Economists call the highest price you would still say yes to your willingness to pay.

Predict first · commit before you click

Write down your number for one large coffee, right now. Then answer this: if I polled all thirty-odd people in this room, what shape would the picture of everyone’s answers make?

In class: hold your number. Do not shout it out — we are about to use it.

A downward staircase. At $1 nearly everyone says yes. At $6 almost nobody does. Every dollar you add, a few more people drop out — and the ones who drop out are the ones for whom the coffee was closest to not being worth it.

That staircase, drawn smooth, is a demand curve. It was never handed down by a textbook. It is made of people’s answers.

Why it is a number and not a feeling: your willingness to pay already has your income, your tastes, the price of the coffee at the place down the road, and how tired you are baked into it. Change any of those and your number changes. Hold that thought — Thursday is entirely about it.

A demand curve is a stack of individual yes-or-no answers, sorted by price.
Instructor notes (9:45 · 8 min)

Get them to physically write the number. “Pen on paper. I’ll wait.” The demonstration is much better when the number is committed before the ladder goes up — otherwise students anchor on their neighbors’ hands.

Take the shape prediction as a show of hands: downward / upward / flat / no pattern. Board the tally. Do not say who is right. Next slide settles it with their data.

§3.1Day 1 · Tuesday

From hands in the air to a demand schedule

A table that shows the quantity buyers would take at each price is a demand schedule. Here is the one a class this size typically produces — yours will differ, and that is fine; the shape is what we are after.

Our class’s demand schedule for large coffee (illustrative — Tuesday’s real numbers replace it)
Price ($/cup)Quantity demanded (cups)Read it out loud
$1.0028“At one dollar a cup, this class would take 28 cups.”
$2.0024“At two dollars a cup, 24 cups.”
$3.0019“At three dollars a cup, 19 cups.”
$4.0013“At four dollars a cup, 13 cups.”
$5.007“At five dollars a cup, 7 cups.”
$6.003“At six dollars a cup, 3 cups.”

Notice the grammar. Every row is an if–then: if the price were $4.00/cup, then the class would collectively take 13 cups. Only one of these rows will ever actually happen. The other five are still true — they are promises about prices we did not face.

A demand schedule is six conditional promises stacked in a table.
Instructor notes (inside the demo block)

§3.1Day 1 · Tuesday

Build #1: turning the table into a curve

Sketch the axes on paper before you click — it costs thirty seconds. Then walk the build one step at a time. (In class we do exactly these five steps on the board.)

  1. Axes and units first — always. Price in dollars per cup on the VERTICAL axis, 0 to $6. Quantity in cups on the HORIZONTAL axis, 0 to 30. If you cannot say the units out loud, the graph is not finished.
  2. Plot the cheap end. At $1.00/cup the class takes 28 cups — far right, near the bottom. At $2.00/cup, 24 cups. Two rows of the table, two dots.
  3. Plot the middle. $3.00/cup → 19 cups; $4.00/cup → 13 cups. The dots are marching up and to the left.
  4. Plot the expensive end. $5.00/cup → 7 cups; $6.00/cup → 3 cups. Six rows, six dots, no line yet.
  5. Connect them and name it. That line is the demand curve, labeled D. Read the trade straight off it: going from $2.00/cup to $4.00/cup costs the class 24 cups − 13 cups = 11 cups of coffee. The curve slopes DOWN — and that is not a convention, it is your own data.
Step 1 of 5
Six rows of a table become one line — and the line slopes down because the table did.
Instructor notes (10:08 · inside the demo block)

§3.1Day 1 · Tuesday

The law of demand — and it really is a law

The law of demand: other things held constant, when the price of a good rises, the quantity demanded falls; when the price falls, the quantity demanded rises. Price and quantity demanded move in opposite directions.

Economists do not use the word law lightly, and there are not many in this course. This one earns it: across goods, countries, and centuries, the relationship holds — and the exceptions are rare enough to be famous. Your own coffee curve just held it too.

✅ What the law says

The direction is reliable: up in price, down in quantity demanded.

✗ What the law does NOT say

It says nothing about how much quantity falls — that is elasticity, and it is Week 4. And it says nothing about what happens if something other than the price changes. That is Thursday.

Checkpoint

The price of gasoline in your neighborhood rises from $4.40/gallon to $4.90/gallon. According to the law of demand, what happens to the quantity of gasoline demanded?

In class: vote first. Fingers up — 1 for falls, 2 for rises, 3 for cannot tell.

It falls — that is exactly what the law of demand predicts, and it is the “other things held constant” clause doing quiet work in the background. People combine errands, carpool, take the trip closer to home.

Notice what we did not say: by how much. A drop of one gallon and a drop of a million gallons are both consistent with the law.

Price up, quantity demanded down. The direction is the law; the size is Week 4.
Instructor notes (10:13 · 6 min)

Run the checkpoint as a real finger vote. It will be near-unanimous — that is fine and fast; you are banking an easy win before the hard vocabulary slide. If anyone votes “cannot tell,” take it seriously and ask why: often they are already sensing the ceteris paribus problem, which is a gift for Thursday. Name them and say “hold that — you are two days ahead.”

§3.1Day 1 · Tuesday

Why it slopes down: you switch, and your money shrinks

“Because things cost more” is not an explanation. Here is what is actually happening, in the two ways economists split it — both of which you felt in your own coffee number.

Predict first · before you read the cards

Picture the ladder from the demonstration. Why did people drop out as the price climbed? Write one reason in your own words — not the textbook’s.

Nearly every answer a class gives is one of the two below. “I’d get tea instead” is reason 1. “I can’t spend that much on coffee” is reason 2. Find your own sentence in the cards, then learn its name.

1 · You switch to something else

When coffee goes to $6/cup, the tea at $2/cup starts looking good. Nothing changed about tea — only the comparison did. Every price rise quietly promotes every alternative.

Economists call this the substitution effect.

2 · Your money does not stretch as far

At $6/cup, a daily coffee eats a bigger share of the same paycheck. You are not poorer on paper, but you are poorer in coffees — so you buy fewer of them, and fewer of some other things too.

Economists call this the income effect.

Where this connects back: both reasons are opportunity cost wearing different hats. Reason 1 is “the next best alternative got relatively cheaper.” Reason 2 is “everything else I could buy with that money got relatively more expensive.” Week 1 is still doing the work.

⚠️ Watch out for: “the demand curve slopes down because people can’t afford it.” That is only half the story — and it is the weaker half. Even someone who could easily afford $6 coffee tends to buy fewer at $6 than at $1, because of reason 1.

You buy less when the price rises because the alternatives got relatively cheaper AND your money buys less.
Instructor notes (10:19 · 6 min)

Harvest first, then label. Ask “why did people drop out as I walked the ladder?” and take three answers before showing the two cards. Students almost always produce both reasons in plain English; your job is to put the names on their sentences, which is far stickier than defining the terms cold.

Do NOT go near indifference curves or Giffen goods. The math ceiling and the survey scope both say no. If a strong student raises “what about goods people buy MORE of when the price rises?” — answer honestly that rare exceptions exist, that they are famous precisely because they are rare, and move on.

§3.1Day 1 · Tuesday

Demand is the curve; quantity demanded is one point on it

This is the vocabulary that decides whether Thursday makes sense. Read it twice.

📈 DEMAND

The whole curve. The entire relationship — every price paired with its quantity. All six rows of the table at once.

“Demand for coffee” = the whole line.

📍 QUANTITY DEMANDED

One point on that curve. One row of the table. Meaningless unless you say the price.

“Quantity of coffee demanded at $3/cup” = 19 cups. One number.

Predict first · commit before you click

“The price of coffee went up, so demand went down.” Is that sentence right or wrong — and how would you fix it?

Wrong — and it is the single most common error in this course. A price change cannot change demand. It moves you to a different point on the same demand curve.

The fix: “The price of coffee went up, so the quantity demanded went down.” Same facts, one word changed, and now it is correct.

If you say this sentence correctly on the quiz, on the exam, and out loud in class, you will have most of Thursday already done.

⚠️ Watch out for: the news gets this wrong constantly — “high prices cut demand” is a stock headline phrase. You are now equipped to notice. That habit is worth points on the Economic Naturalist assignments later in the term.

Demand is the curve. Quantity demanded is a point on it. A price change moves the point, never the curve.

§3.1Day 1 · Tuesday

From one buyer to a whole market

Your coffee number is your demand. The curve we built is the market demand for this room. Getting from one to the other is simple addition — but you add across, at each price, not down.

Checkpoint

At $4.00/cup, what is the market quantity demanded for these three buyers?

3 cups. Maya 1 cup + Devon 0 cups + Rosa 2 cups = 3 cups. Devon contributing zero is not a mistake — at $4.00/cup he is simply out of the market, and “out” is a perfectly good quantity.

Market demand is every individual’s demand, added across at each price.
Instructor notes (10:35 · 10 min)

§3.1Day 1 · Tuesday

Price goes on the vertical axis — the quirk that bites in Week 4

In every math class you have ever taken, the thing that does the causing goes on the horizontal axis. In economics, price goes on the vertical axis even though price is usually doing the causing.

This is a real exception — a habit more than a century old that economics never got around to fixing. Every demand and supply graph you will ever see — in this course, in a newspaper, in an economics degree — is drawn this way.

✅ Do this

Price ($/unit) up the side. Quantity (units) along the bottom. Every time, without thinking about it.

✗ Not this

Price along the bottom because it “feels” like the cause. Your graph will be readable to nobody, including you in Week 4.

Practical consequence: when a demand curve shifts, it shifts sideways — left or right — because quantity is the horizontal axis. That is why Thursday’s language is “shifts right” and never “shifts up.” The axis convention and the vocabulary are the same fact.

Price is always on the vertical axis. Shifts are therefore always sideways.
Instructor notes (10:45 · 7 min — cut first if the demo ran long)

Short and firm. The payoff line is the last one: it is why Thursday says “right/left” not “up/down,” and students who get this now make one fewer error later. If you cut this slide, say one sentence: “price on the vertical axis, always — it’s in the deck.”

WARM-UPDay 2 · Thursday

Tuesday’s cliffhanger, resolved

Tuesday we left this hanging: everyone gets a raise, coffee stays $3.00/cup, and our curve insists the class takes 19 cups. Our curve is now wrong. Why?

Because a demand curve is drawn under a promise — the promise that nothing except the price of this good is changing. Break the promise and the curve is no longer describing the world. It has to be redrawn.

The price of coffee changes

The promise holds. You move to a different point on the same curve.

A movement along the curve.

Anything else changes

The promise breaks. The old curve is retired and a new one is drawn beside it.

A shift of the curve.

A demand curve is only valid while its promise holds. Break the promise, redraw the curve.

§3.2Day 2 · Thursday

One rule. Memorize it today, use it all semester.

Own price changed → MOVE along the curve.
Anything else changed → SHIFT the whole curve.

That is the whole thing. The reason it is hard is not the rule — it is that you have to notice which variable moved before you can apply it, and news stories rarely say so plainly.

The procedure, in the order you must do it:

  1. Name the variable that changed. Out loud, in words. Not “the market changed” — what changed?
  2. Ask one question: is that variable the price of this exact good?
  3. Yes → movement along. No → shift.
  4. Only then pick a direction, and say it as “more/less at every price.”

⚠️ Watch out for: reaching for the direction first. Students who guess “right” or “left” before naming the variable miss far more of them than students who name the variable first. Naming it costs you three seconds.

Instructor notes (9:38 · 7 min)

Write the two-line rule on the board and leave it for the rest of the hour. Do not elaborate — the six shifters and the drill do the elaborating. Say the name-the-variable discipline is non-negotiable and that you will be asking “what moved?” every single time for the next four weeks. Then actually do it.

§3.2Day 2 · Thursday

Build #2: the raise, drawn

Same class, same coffee, same prices — everybody just got a raise. Sketch what you think happens before you click. (In class: you draw it on paper first; I draw it second.)

  1. Start from Tuesday’s curve. D0 — the one this class built from its own bids. At $3.00/cup it says 19 cups.
  2. Ask the new question at ONE price. Hold the price at $3.00/cup and ask again now that everyone has more income. The answer comes back 25 cups, not 19.
  3. Now ask it at EVERY price. At $1.00/cup: 34 instead of 28. At $6.00/cup: 9 instead of 3. More at every single price — that is the signature of a shift.
  4. Draw the new curve through the new answers. D1, drawn solid — it is just as real as D0. Both curves stay on the picture so you can see what changed.
  5. Name it correctly. Demand increased: the curve shifted right. Not “up.” Not “quantity demanded rose.” The price never moved — income did.
Step 1 of 5
A shift is not one point moving. It is every point moving, in the same direction, at once.
Instructor notes (9:45 · 10 min)

Draw-it-yourself first: “on your paper, shift Tuesday’s curve for a class-wide raise. Thirty seconds.” Circulate. You are looking for one specific error — students who move the DOT along the old curve instead of drawing a new one. Find two, and ask permission to show one on the document camera; it is the most useful wrong answer of the semester.

Step 3 is the beat that matters. Say it as you click: “not one price. Every price. That is what makes it a shift.”

§3.2Day 2 · Thursday

The two pictures, side by side

Reading panel (a)

  • One curve on the picture
  • Two labeled points on that one curve
  • The cause was the price of gasoline itself
  • Say: “quantity demanded fell”

Reading panel (b)

  • Two curves on the picture
  • Same price, two different quantities
  • The cause was something other than the price
  • Say: “demand increased”

The fastest tell on an exam: count the curves. One curve on the picture means a movement. Two curves means a shift. It is not a substitute for naming the variable, but it will catch you when you are rushing.

Instructor notes (9:55 · 5 min)

Show the two panels and interrogate rather than explain: “How many curves in (a)? In (b)? So what changed in each?” Cold-call two students. The count-the-curves tell works, and students remember it — but say out loud that it is a check, not the method.

§3.2Day 2 · Thursday

Say it as “more or less at every price” — the phrasing that cannot go wrong

“The curve went up” is ambiguous — up the page, or up in quantity? Those are different directions. Economists solved this by describing shifts in terms of quantity at a given price, and once you adopt that phrasing most direction errors disappear.

The same event, said three ways
Instead of…Say…Why it is safer
“Demand went up”“Demand increased — more at every price” “Up” could mean up the price axis. “More at every price” cannot be misread.
“The curve moved right”“The curve shifted right — more at every price” Right is correct, but pairing it with the meaning keeps you from memorizing a direction you cannot justify.
“People want less”“Demand decreased — less at every price” “Want” leaves out ability to pay, and leaves out the price entirely.
Checkpoint

A campus survey finds students are drinking less coffee than they used to, at every price. Which is the correct sentence? (a) quantity demanded fell   (b) demand decreased   (c) the price fell

(b) demand decreased. The giveaway is the phrase at every price — that is the definition of a shift. Answer (a) would need a price change to cause it, and nothing here says the price moved at all.

“More at every price” and “less at every price” are the only two shift phrases you need.
Instructor notes (10:00 · 5 min — folded into the six-shifters block)

Drill the phrase chorally once. It feels silly and it works: “shifts right means…” / class: “more at every price.” Twice. Then use those exact words for the rest of the semester so the phrasing stays alive.

§3.2Day 2 · Thursday

The six things that shift a demand curve

Predict first · before you look at the picture

Hold coffee at $3.00/cup. What, other than that price, could change how many cups this class takes? List as many as you can in thirty seconds, then check them against the six below.

Most lists hit three or four of the six. Income and tastes come easily; the price of tea or creamer, and what people expect prices to do next week, are the ones usually missed — and the ones the quiz leans on.

⚠️ The most important thing on this slide is what is NOT on it. The price of the good itself is absent, deliberately. If you ever find yourself listing “the price went up” as a reason a curve shifted, you have made the error this whole module exists to prevent.

Six shifters. The good’s own price is not one of them.
Instructor notes (10:00 · 14 min for this and the four detail slides)

Do NOT read the six aloud — that is a waste of fourteen minutes and they have the picture. Instead, name each one and immediately ask the room for a coffee example: “income — what would that look like for our coffee curve?” Six shifters, six student-generated examples, board them beside the curve. Much stickier than the list.

Spend the real minutes on the two they will get wrong: related goods (next slide) and expectations. Income, number of buyers, and population composition are nearly self-evident once named.

§3.2Day 2 · Thursday

Shifter 1 · Income — and the good that goes the wrong way

For most things, more income means more buying at every price. Economists call those normal goods. But some goods you buy less of when you can afford better — those are inferior goods, and the name is about your wallet, not the quality.

Predict first

Which of these is most likely an inferior good for a typical Palomar student — and why? (a) instant ramen   (b) concert tickets   (c) a used textbook   (d) both (a) and (c)

(d) — both instant ramen and used textbooks. When your income rises you tend to buy fewer of both: you eat out more, and you buy the new book instead of hunting the used one. Concert tickets go the other way — more income, more concerts — so they are a normal good.

“Inferior” is not an insult. Ramen is delicious. The label only describes which way the curve moves when income changes.

Normal good: income up, curve right. Inferior good: income up, curve left.
Instructor notes (inside the six-shifters block)

The name is the whole obstacle. Say it directly: “inferior does not mean bad. It means you buy less of it when you get richer. That is the entire definition.” Ask for one inferior good from their own life — bus passes, generic cereal, and roommates all come up, and the roommate answer is a good laugh that happens to be correct.

§3.2Day 2 · Thursday

Shifter 2 · The price of a different good

This is the shifter students miss most, because a price changed — it just was not this good’s price. Keep the rule honest: the price of a different good is “something else,” so it shifts.

🔄 SUBSTITUTES — used instead of

Tea and coffee. Uber and the bus. Chicken and beef.

Tea gets more expensive → people switch toward coffee → demand for coffee shifts RIGHT.

Substitute price and your demand move the same way.

🔗 COMPLEMENTS — used together with

Coffee and creamer. Gasoline and SUVs. Printers and ink.

Creamer gets more expensive → the whole coffee ritual costs more → demand for coffee shifts LEFT.

Complement price and your demand move in opposite ways.

Checkpoint

The price of gasoline falls sharply. What happens in the market for large SUVs — and is it a shift or a movement?

In class: vote, pair up, re-vote.

Demand for SUVs shifts RIGHT — a shift, not a movement. Name the variable first: what changed was the price of gasoline, not the price of SUVs. Gasoline and SUVs are complements, so cheaper gas makes owning an SUV cheaper overall, and buyers want more of them at every SUV price.

The trap: seeing the word “price” and reflexively answering “movement.” Always ask whose price.

A price changed — but not this good’s price. That is still a shift.
Instructor notes (inside the six-shifters block — the re-vote site)

This is one of the classic error sites, so run the FULL peer-instruction cycle here: vote → pair-discuss sixty seconds → re-vote → resolve. Expect the first vote to split roughly evenly on shift-vs-movement and the second to land near 80% correct. Say the numbers out loud afterwards — showing students that the discussion moved the room is what sells the method for the rest of the term.

§3.2Day 2 · Thursday

Shifters 3–6 · Tastes, who is buying, and what buyers expect

Tastes first, because it needs the least explaining: coffee comes into fashion, demand shifts right — and no price appears anywhere in the story. The other three deserve a closer look.

👥 Number of buyers

Palomar enrollment jumps 15% this fall → more people in the campus coffee market → demand shifts right. Nothing about any individual’s taste changed; there are simply more of them.

📊 Composition of the population

Not just how many, but who. An aging population raises demand for hearing aids and lowers it for tricycles — even if the total number of people is flat.

Predict first

The cart posts a sign: “Prices go up 50 cents on Monday.” Today is Thursday. What happens to the demand for coffee today — right, left, or nothing yet?

Right, today. Today’s price has not moved, so this is not a movement. What changed is what buyers expect — and expecting a higher price later makes people buy more now, at every price today.

🔮 Expectations — the one that feels backwards

Buyers act on what they think is coming, not just what is true now.

  • Hurricane forecast on the news → people buy bottled water today → demand today shifts right.
  • Everyone hears next year’s phone will be cheaper → they wait → demand today shifts left.

The tell: expecting a higher future price shifts today’s demand right. It feels backwards until you notice the sentence is about two different days.

⚠️ Watch out for: confusing an expected future price with the current price. A change in the price today is a movement along today’s curve. A change in what people expect tomorrow’s price to be is a shift of today’s curve. Same word, two different days, two different answers.

Changed tastes, more buyers, different buyers, or changed beliefs about tomorrow — all shifts.
Instructor notes (inside the six-shifters block)

Expectations is the second error site. The two-different-days framing is the fix — write “TODAY” and “TOMORROW” on the board and point at them while you say it. A live example works well if one is available: any current news story about people stockpiling ahead of an announced price rise or tariff. Check it the morning of class; do not use a stale one.

§3.2Day 2 · Thursday

Ceteris paribus: the fine print on every curve you will ever draw

Ceteris paribus (Latin, “other things being equal”): the assumption that nothing else relevant is changing. Every demand curve is drawn under it. So is every supply curve you will meet next week.

It sounds like a technicality. It is actually the reason the whole method works. A demand curve relates two variables — price and quantity. The real world has hundreds moving at once. Ceteris paribus is how economists take one thing at a time, on purpose, and then put the answers back together.

How it is used in practice: if the price of coffee rises and incomes fall in the same month, you do not throw up your hands. You do it twice — once for the price change (a movement along), once for the income change (a shift) — and then combine. That two-step habit is exactly what Week 4’s four-step method formalizes.

Honest limitation, stated plainly: ceteris paribus is an assumption, not a fact. The real world rarely holds still. That is a real weakness of the model, and pretending otherwise would be bad economics — the model earns its keep by being useful, not by being true.

One thing at a time, on purpose — then put the pieces back together.
Instructor notes (10:40 · 4 min)

Keep the honest-limitation beat. It is the fact-vs-opinion / assumptions discipline from COR objective 1, and it costs thirty seconds. Students trust a model more, not less, when you tell them where it is weak.

Sixteen minutes, in pairs, with named roles. Every round: name the variable → decide shift or movement → if a shift, say the direction as “more/less at every price.”

§3.1–3.2Day 2 · Thursday

Gasoline — the example the course is graded on

One of the two official learning outcomes for ECON 100 names gasoline specifically. So we do it properly once, now, with a full demand schedule.

Predict first · the exam-style question

It is June. Gas prices rise about 10 cents/gallon above the winter low, every year, like clockwork — and people still drive more in summer than in winter. Explain that using this graph. Which effect is a movement and which is a shift?

Two separate things are happening, and you have to say both.

1 — The shift. Summer arrives: school is out, people take road trips. That is a change in tastes and circumstances, not in the price of gas, so the whole demand curve shifts right — more gasoline demanded at every price.

2 — The movement. On that new curve, the higher summer price pulls buyers back along it, reducing quantity demanded somewhat.

Net result: people buy more gas in summer despite paying more per gallon. The shift outweighs the movement. Neither half alone can explain it — and that is exactly why the two ideas have different names.

Real events usually contain a shift AND a movement. Name both, in that order.
Instructor notes (10:30 · 10 min)

This is the SLO 1 rehearsal — treat it as such and say so: “this is the kind of question the college grades this course on.”

Build it on the board in the two moves, shift first, movement second. Students who try to do it in one move tie themselves in knots; the discipline of doing the shift first and the movement second on the NEW curve is the transferable skill and it previews Week 4’s four-step method directly.

[Current-fact note] The seasonal 10-cent pattern and the schedule are from OpenStax and are illustrative, not today’s prices. If a student asks what gas costs right now, look it up together — that is a good thirty seconds, not an interruption.

WATCH OUTDay 2 · Thursday

The three traps — all three are on the quiz

Trap 1 · “Price went up, so demand fell.”
A price change never changes demand. Say quantity demanded. This is the most-missed idea in the course, and it is worth more points across the semester than any other single sentence.

Trap 2 · “A price changed, so it must be a movement.”
Only if it is this good’s price. The price of a substitute or a complement is somebody else’s price, and it shifts your curve. Ask whose price before you answer.

Trap 3 · “Shifts up / shifts down.”
Curves shift right (more at every price) or left (less at every price). Because quantity is on the horizontal axis, sideways is the only direction that means anything.

Checkpoint · all three at once

“Beef got expensive last year, so demand for chicken went up.” Two things are wrong with that sentence. What are they, and how do you fix it?

Actually — only one thing is wrong. Read it carefully. Beef and chicken are substitutes, so a beef price rise really does shift the demand for chicken to the right. Saying “demand for chicken went up” is correct, because the price that changed was not chicken’s.

The one real fix: “went up” is loose. Say “demand for chicken increased — more chicken demanded at every price.”

This one is a deliberate trick, and it is the difference between memorizing “price = movement” and actually applying the rule. Name the variable first, every time.

Instructor notes (10:44 · 5 min)

The checkpoint is a deliberate trap — the stem says “two things are wrong” and only one is. Let them fall into it; the recovery is the lesson. Expect groans. Say: “I set that up on purpose, and I will do it again on the exam. The defense is the same every time: name the variable that moved.”

HISTORYDay 2 · Thursday

Where this idea came from: markets came first

Markets are older than economics by several thousand years. People were haggling in Mesopotamian grain markets around 3000 BCE, and every one of them understood in their bones that asking a higher price meant selling to fewer buyers. Nobody needed a curve to know it.

What was missing

Not the knowledge — the picture. For most of history there was no way to write down “how much would be bought at each possible price” and reason about it. Arguments about prices were arguments about morality: what a just price ought to be.

What the curve changed

Drawing demand as a schedule turned an argument about fairness into a question you could answer with evidence: at what price, how much? That does not settle the fairness question — it just stops the two questions from being confused with each other.

Coming in Week 4: the person who put demand and supply on the same diagram and made them settle a price together was Alfred Marshall, in 1890. He is the reason you have been looking at only half a picture all week — and the reason Week 4 will feel like a payoff.

People always knew price and quantity moved oppositely. The curve made it something you could argue about with evidence.
Instructor notes (10:49 · 3 min — cut to one sentence if behind)

COR economic-history thread (01 §4, strands 1 and 3). Keep it to three minutes. The payoff line is the just-price contrast: students find it interesting, and it reinforces the positive-vs-normative distinction from Module 1 without another lecture on it. Marshall gets his proper cameo in Week 4.
